    PhD Mental Health Crisis — Longitudinal Evidence¶

    Comprehensive population-wide longitudinal study (N=20,085 PhD students) demonstrating that PhD studies cause significant mental health deterioration, with a 39.5% increase in psychiatric medication use by year 5, recovering after graduation.

    Core Finding: Causal Impact¶

    Baseline Comparison (Pre-PhD)¶

    Metric PhD Students Control (Master's) General Population
    Psychiatric medication (1 year before PhD) 7% 6% 10%
    Parent with PhD 12% 6% 2%
    Parent psychiatric medication 30% 28% 32%

    Critical insight: Prospective PhD students do not have pre-existing mental health vulnerabilities or disproportionate family histories. The crisis is caused by the program itself, not selection.

    Timeline of Mental Health Deterioration¶

    Event Study Results¶

    Year Relative to PhD Psychiatric Medication Use Change from Baseline
    Year -1 (baseline) Reference 0%
    Year 0-2 Initial increase Early deterioration
    Year 3-4 Continued increase Progressive worsening
    Year 5 Peak +39.5% (+2.5 percentage points)
    After Year 5 Decline Recovery during graduation
    7-10 years after Still elevated Long-term effects persist

    Key observations:

    1. No adaptation period: Deterioration starts immediately, continues progressively
    2. Cumulative stress: 40% increase by year 5, not abrupt spike then plateau
    3. Recovery possible: Notable decline after graduation suggests situational/transient effect
    4. Persistent elevation: Still elevated 7-10 years later

    Hospitalizations: Severe Cases¶

    Secondary Outcome¶

    Hospitalizations for mental/behavioral disorders: - Base rate: 0.24% pre-PhD (very low) - Finding: Similar pattern to medication, larger relative effect size - Includes: Major depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, substance abuse, self-harm

    Significance: - Hospitalizations capture acute/severe conditions - Less influenced by healthcare-seeking propensity - Confirms genuine mental health deterioration, not just increased help-seeking

    Alternative Explanations Ruled Out¶

    1. Correlated Shocks¶

    Concern: PhD start coincides with other life events (graduation, job, relocation)

    Test: Event study on control group around university graduation

    Result: No increase in psychiatric medication around graduation

    Conclusion: Estimates not driven by correlated life transitions

    2. Healthcare-Seeking Behavior¶

    Concern: Estimates reflect increased willingness to seek care, not worse mental health

    Tests and Results:

    Test Finding Conclusion
    Hospitalizations Similar pattern, larger effect Genuine deterioration
    Other medication classes Modest increases, smaller than psych meds Not general healthcare increase
    Other hospitalizations General increase, but mental health larger Mental health specifically affected

    Conclusion: Reflects underlying mental health deterioration, not just help-seeking behavior

    3. General Health Trends¶

    Test: Analyzed 6 largest pharmacological groups (ATC A, D, G, J, M, R)

    Finding: Modest increases in several classes, but psychiatric medications consistently larger

    Conclusion: PhD studies specifically affect mental health

    Robustness Checks¶

    All findings robust across:

    1. Alternative event study methods
    2. Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024)
    3. Sun & Abraham (2021)

    4. Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021)

    5. Sample restrictions

    6. Removed Swedish master's degree requirement

    7. Alternative activity level thresholds

    8. Alternative control groups

    9. Stacked Diff-in-Diff comparing to future graduate students

    10. Sample composition

    11. Balanced panels (7 years before/after)
    12. Splits by master's-to-PhD timing

    Heterogeneity: Who Is Affected?¶

    Overall Pattern¶

    Effect widespread across nearly all subgroups (medical/health sciences excepted). Magnitude varies.

    Individual-Level Factors¶

    Factor Higher Risk Lower Risk Effect Size
    Gender Men Women 28% vs 16% (p=0.011)
    Birthplace Foreign-born Sweden-born 36% vs 17% (p=0.011)
    Age <29 years ≥29 years 34% vs 14% (p<0.001)
    Relocation Relocated Stayed 39% vs 20% (p=0.045)
    Funding PhD employment Scholarships/company 27% vs 0-16%
    Leadership ability Low High 39% vs 13% (p=0.031)

    No significant differences by: - Parental education (whether parent has PhD) - Parental mental health history - Marital status at PhD entry - Having children at PhD entry

    Important nuance: Some differences reflect baseline rates. Absolute increases in percentage points often similar across groups.

    Institutional-Level Factors¶

    Characteristic Risk Factor Protective Factor Risk Ratio
    Professor-to-PhD ratio Low (≤0.17) Higher 39.3% increase (low)
    Female professors Low (≤10%) Higher 38.7% increase (low)
    Female PhD students Low (≤38%) Higher 43.3% increase (low)
    Time to graduation Long Short Worse outcomes

    Surprising finding: Post-graduation labor market outcomes NOT strongly associated: - Unemployment rates - Probability of staying in academia - Average wages - Wage premium for PhD

    The Medical & Health Sciences Exception¶

    Finding¶

    Only field with NO significant increase in psychiatric medication use

    Potential Explanations¶

    1. Higher pre-PhD stress: Medical education already very stressful
    2. Different support structures: Better mentorship or supervision
    3. Healthcare access: Colleagues' expertise, easier access to care
    4. Earlier diagnosis: Lower stigma, higher mental health literacy
    5. Selection: Students have higher stress tolerance, leadership scores
    6. Employment context: Many employed by hospitals alongside PhD

    Observed Differences¶

    Characteristic Medical/Health Sciences Other Fields
    Stress tolerance Higher Lower
    Leadership ability Higher Lower
    Parents with PhD Higher share Lower share
    Relocation Less frequent More frequent
    Time to degree Shortest Longer

    Mechanisms and Policy Implications¶

    What Drives the Deterioration?¶

    Institutional factors with strongest association:

    1. Low faculty-to-student ratios: Inadequate supervision
    2. Long time to graduation: Prolonged uncertainty
    3. Low female representation: Potentially isolating norms or climates
    4. Relocation: Social disconnection, lack of support networks

    Individual factors with strongest association:

    1. Younger students: Less life experience?
    2. Foreign-born: Cultural adaptation stress, language barriers
    3. Low leadership ability: Less ability to navigate system?

    Recommended Interventions¶

    For Universities:

    1. Increase supervision resources: Raise faculty-to-student ratios
    2. Clear milestones: Provide structured program timelines
    3. Gender diversity: Promote balance for equity AND well-being
    4. Relocation support: Stronger onboarding, peer mentoring, housing
    5. Early intervention: Mental health support from PhD start, not just later

    For Students:

    1. Awareness: Vulnerability increases during PhD
    2. Not personal failure: Deterioration is normal/common, not weakness
    3. Recovery possible: After graduation, not permanent
    4. Proactive care: Build support networks, seek help early

    For Policymakers:

    1. Institutional reform: Structural problems require structural solutions
    2. Selection concerns: PhD programs may select for resilience over aptitude
    3. Researcher pipeline: Mental health impacts academic productivity

    Comparison to International Evidence¶

    Prevalence Comparisons¶

    Population Treatment/Medication
    Swedish PhD students (2016) 13.5%
    US economics PhD students (2017-18) 14.9% treatment
    Political science PhD students (2020) 10-13.5% treatment
    PhD students (symptoms - meta-analysis) 24% depression/anxiety

    Alignment: Screening tools overestimate prevalence by ~2x vs clinical interviews. Our administrative estimates align with international surveys.

    Context: Related Research¶

    Survey-Based Evidence¶

    Study Sample Mental Health Prevalence
    Levecque et al. (2017) 3,659 Dutch graduate students 32% at risk of psychiatric disorder
    Evans et al. (2018) International graduate students Similar or higher rates
    Satinsky et al. (2021) Meta-analysis, >23,000 PhD 24% depression, 17% anxiety
    Bolotnyy et al. (2022) 513 US economics PhD 25% moderate/severe depression/anxiety
    Macchi et al. (2023) 556 European economics PhD 35% moderate/severe depression/anxiety

    Advantages of Longitudinal Administrative Study¶

    1. Objective: Prescription records, not self-reports
    2. Population-wide: No selection bias
    3. Causal: Pre-post comparison controls for time-invariant confounding
    4. Severity: Captures both mild (medication) and severe (hospitalizations)

    Limitations¶

    1. Underestimation: Only captures individuals who seek care
    2. Help-seeking bias: Treatment rates vary by demographics
    3. Women, highly educated, Caucasians, unmarried: higher treatment
    4. May explain some heterogeneity (men, foreign-born)
    5. May explain medical sciences exception (lower stigma)
    6. Stigma: Key barrier to care (Clement et al., 2015)
    7. Swedish context: Universal healthcare, may not generalize globally
    8. Diagnostic specificity: Don't capture specific diagnoses, only treatment class

    Comparison: PhD Studies vs. Parental Death¶

    Benchmarked against sudden loss of a parent: - PhD impact: Larger in relative terms - PhD impact: More persistent over time - Parental death: Acute trauma, different dynamics

    Interpretation: PhD studies function as a chronic, sustained stressor with longer-lasting impact than acute traumatic events.
