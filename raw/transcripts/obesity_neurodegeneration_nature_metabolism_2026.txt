The parallel rise in obesity and neurological disorders suggests a potential 
mechanistic link between these two conditions. Midlife obesity is a 
well-established risk factor for cognitive decline and neurological disorders; 
however, the underlying mechanisms and cause-and-effect relationship 
remain poorly defined, in part owing to limited spatiotemporal resolution 
in existing studies and the absence of a unifying framework. Recent studies 
have begun to uncover pathways linking obesity to neurodegenerative 
vulnerability. First, we discuss how obesity induces brain-wide 
reprogramming across neural, metabolic and vascular systems including 
neurovascular coupling, blood–brain barrier integrity, cerebrospinal fluid 
dynamics and myelination. By systematically examining the disparate 
mechanisms for each condition, a more cohesive picture emerges, revealing 
progressive neurodegeneration driven by chronic metabolic overload. 
Lastly, we propose a framework that integrates these different mechanisms 
with both spatial and temporal specificity.
“Every solution to a problem raises new unsolved problems.” – 
Karl Popper
The industrialization of the global food system began with 
a well-intentioned goal: to combat hunger and food scarcity on 
a global scale. Over time, this effort led to the mass production of 
ultra-processed, calorie-dense and highly palatable foods. These prod-
ucts contributed to an increased food security and longer lifespans but 
also introduced new challenges. The modern nutritional landscape now 
fuels chronic diseases that erode long-term metabolic, cardiovascular 
and cognitive health1. For the first time in human history, overnutrition, 
rather than scarcity, has become a leading driver of disease. The rapid 
surge in obesity places immense pressure on public health systems 
to develop prevention and treatment strategies. Current evidence 
indicates that increased calorie intake, rather than reduced physical 
activity, is considered the root cause of the global obesity epidemic2.
Alongside obesity, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus 
(T2DM), metabolic dysfunction and neurodegenerative disorders 
has grown at an alarming rate. This parallel rise has sparked renewed 
interest in how chronic exposure to hyperpalatable, metabolically 
disruptive diets may rewire the brain’s homeostatic and reward circuits, 
reinforcing cycles of overeating and physiological dysregulation3. 
The epidemiological trajectory suggests that metabolic overload and 
neurological vulnerability are not isolated phenomena but may reflect 
shared biological mechanisms4.
A growing body of evidence supports this view. Chronic meta-
bolic overload alters central circuits that maintain energy balance, 
disrupts neurovascular and inflammatory homeostasis and acceler-
ates brain ageing5. Obesity is consistently associated with reduced 
cognitive performance and brain volume, and imaging signatures 
of accelerated brain ageing6,7. Unsurprisingly, these accumulating 
alterations heighten the risk for neurological disorders8. Importantly, 
obesity is not a uniform state. Visceral adiposity, defined as the excess 
intra-abdominal fat surrounding internal organs, is the strongest pre-
dictor of metabolic dysfunction, more so than total fat mass, and 
is linked to greater structural and functional disruption within the 
brain9,10. The mechanisms discussed in this Perspective, including 
inflammatory, vascular-barrier and fluid-structural pathways, primarily 
reflect the consequences of visceral obesity.
Yet a shared mechanism does not automatically imply causality 
or a unified disease model. To understand how chronic metabolic 
overload contributes to progressive neural decline, both reductionist 
and systems-level approaches are needed. This Perspective adopts 
that dual framework: dissecting how obesity perturbs specific cel-
lular, vascular and inflammatory processes (reductionist view), while

The domino effect of neurodegeneration
Understanding how neurocircuit dysfunction unfolds in the context of 
obesity requires moving beyond the traditional neuron-centric model, 
where neurons are seen as the sole drivers of brain function. Instead, 
a more comprehensive framework is emerging in which glial, vascu-
lar and immune cells have indispensable and interconnected roles in 
maintaining neural stability27. Within this expanded view, the brain 
is recognized as a dynamic neuro–glial–vascular ecosystem whose 
homeostasis depends on coordinated communication across multiple 
cell types. Chronic metabolic overload disrupts this balance, provok-
ing time-dependent and region-specific shifts in cellular states. By 
examining how signals propagate across these diverse populations, 
we can begin to identify network-level vulnerabilities that predispose 
specific brain circuits to neurodegeneration.
Recent advances in neuroscience, including single-cell and spatial 
omics, genetically defined cell-type-specific targeting and intravital 
imaging, have enabled high-resolution mapping of cell–cell interac-
tions within the brain28,29. These tools make it possible to move beyond 
isolated pathways and towards an integrate understanding of how 
obesity perturbs the broader architecture of brain function. The fol-
lowing section introduces an emerging paradigm that shifts from a 
neuron-centric view and integrates the entirety of the neuro–glial–
vascular unit (NVU), capturing the microenvironmental complexity 
and heterogeneity that shape circuit resilience.
A well-characterized example of NVU-mediated communication 
is neurovascular coupling (NVC) or functional hyperaemia, in which 
active neurons signal to nearby blood vessels to increase local cerebral 
blood flow (CBF)30. Yet neuron–vascular interactions extend far beyond 
NVC. Neuronal activity also drives vascular remodelling, modulates 
glymphatic clearance of metabolic waste and influences oligodendro-
cyte maturation and adaptive myelination31–34. These processes are 
deeply interdependent, forming an intricately coordinated network in 
which perturbations at any node can reverberate through the system. 
Disruption of this finely tuned coordination has profound implications 
for both local circuit function and global network integrity.
At the systems level, interventions such as brain-wide gamma 
stimulation illustrate the integrative nature of NVU function: these 
oscillatory manipulations simultaneously promote vascular remodel-
ling, glial plasticity, waste clearance and myelin adaptation35–38. Such 
findings underscore that brain health is inherently multicellular, sus-
tained not by neurons alone but by the emergent properties of a coor-
dinated, brain-wide cellular ensemble. When challenged by chronic 
metabolic overload, this intricate system becomes increasingly fragile, 
initiating a cascade of maladaptive responses that propagate through 
neurons, glia and vasculature in a progressive, circuit-specific manner.
In the following sections, we adopt a reductionist approach to dis-
sect how obesity alters the major components of brain homeostasis. We 
then integrate these insights into a cohesive systems-level framework, 
proposing a convergent-cascade model in which dysfunction of the 
NVU represents a central pathway linking chronic overnutrition to 
progressive neurodegeneration.
Contrasting frameworks of neurodegenerative 
vulnerability in obesity: cascade versus 
convergence
Although obesity is known to alter both brain structure and function, 
integrating these diverse effects into a unified mechanistic model 
remains challenging. The current understanding is that the brain, 
particularly the hypothalamus, defends a biologically stable level of 
adiposity through long-term homeostatic mechanisms that match 
energy intake to expenditure39. In obesity, this defended level shifts 
upwards rather than collapsing entirely, and this resetting is accompa-
nied by hypothalamic inflammation and gliosis40,41. Because these exact 
glial and immune mechanisms are implicated in neurodegenerative 
diseases42, such persistent adaptations represent only one of several 
assembling these alterations into an integrated model of how meta-
bolic stress accelerates neurodegeneration (systems-level view). 
Throughout this Perspective, we clearly differentiate between pre-
clinical and clinical evidence and explicitly note where human data 
remain limited.
As we uncover how metabolism sculpts the brain at the molecu-
lar, cellular and systems levels, a holistic picture emerges: metabolic 
health is not only about the body; it is fundamentally about the brain. 
Understanding this relationship is essential for designing the next 
generation of metabolic therapies, in which protecting the brain is as 
central as controlling weight or glucose.
Obesity-driven vulnerability to cognitive decline 
and neurodegeneration
Although obesity is associated with measurable cognitive impairments, 
the extent to which these changes reflect actual neurodegeneration 
processes remains unresolved. Obesity and neurodegeneration do 
not constitute a strict clinical continuum. Most individuals with obe-
sity will never develop a neurodegenerative disorder, and many peo-
ple with Alzheimer’s disease or related conditions have no history of 
obesity. Nevertheless, epidemiological evidence consistently links 
metabolic dysfunction to an increased risk of accelerated cognitive 
ageing11, suggesting that obesity can act as a vulnerability factor in 
certain individuals.
Large-scale neuroimaging cohorts have reported overlapping 
patterns of brain atrophy in obesity and Alzheimer’s disease, particu-
larly in temporo-parietal and hippocampal regions12. Dietary patterns 
provide further insight: higher consumption of ultra-processed foods, 
rich in refined carbohydrates, fats and additives, is associated with 
accelerated cognitive decline13, whereas diets enriched in whole foods, 
such as vegetables, fruit and whole grains, correlate with greater corti-
cal and hippocampal volumes14. This relationship appears especially 
salient in adolescence, a period of heightened neurodevelopmental 
plasticity, when childhood obesity has become a global health con-
cern15. Cognitive deficits, such as sluggish cognitive tempo, are more 
prevalent in children with obesity than in their healthy peers16, rais-
ing concern that early-life metabolic stress may reshape long-term 
neurocognitive trajectories. Maternal obesity compounds this vul-
nerability, with increased rates of neuropsychiatric and neurodevelop-
ment disorders, documented across offspring cohorts17,18. Together, 
these data underscore the urgency of public health strategies aimed 
at mitigating childhood and maternal obesity to reduce downstream 
neurological burden19.
While epidemiological associations highlight the scope of the 
problem, experimental models have begun to reveal the biological 
pathways that may causally link obesity to neural vulnerability. Pre-
clinical studies provide more mechanistic consistency than human 
data, yet their translational relevance must be interpreted cautiously. 
In mouse models, obesity induces glial senescence within neurogenic 
niches, and clearance of these senescent cells restores neurogenesis 
and ameliorates anxiety-like behaviour20. Obesity also heightens neu-
roinflammation, promotes lipid accumulation in glial and endothelial 
populations, and compromises blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, 
collectively sensitizing the brain to injury and degeneration21. Nota-
bly, metabolic overload exacerbates cognitive decline across diverse 
models of Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease22–24, suggesting 
that obesity can accelerate pathogenic processes even when it is not 
the primary driver of disease.
Therefore, emerging evidence supports a view in which obesity 
exerts widespread systemic effects, including metabolic, inflamma-
tory, vascular and hormonal effects, that converge on the brain to lower 
its threshold for dysfunction4,25,26. This Perspective synthesizes these 
insights to examine how chronic metabolic overload reshapes cerebral 
architecture and function, increasing susceptibility to cognitive decline 
and neurological disease.

Two prevailing conceptual models have been used to interpret 
neurodegenerative vulnerability in obesity. The ‘cascade hypothesis’ 
posits that a single initiating insult, such as inflammation, triggers a 
sequential chain of downstream events (‘domino effect’) culminating 
in cognitive decline. The ‘convergence hypothesis’ suggests instead 
that multiple pathological processes (vascular, immune, metabolic, 
structural) act in parallel on shared neural substrates, ultimately con-
verging on degenerative outcome. Canonical examples are the amyloid 
cascade hypothesis and the two-hit model43,44. Both frameworks cap-
ture elements of disease progression yet oversimplify the complexity 
and interdependence of chronic metabolic injury.
In obesity, early hypothalamic inflammation has been inter-
preted as a potential cascade trigger. Rodents exposed to a high-fat 
diet (HFD) for as little as 3 days exhibit reactive gliosis and neuronal 
injury in the mediobasal hypothalamus. This phenomenon is also 
observed in humans with obesity45,46. Early immune activation perturbs 
NVC and CBF, processes tightly regulated by glial and endothelial 
cells47,48. Disruption of NVU communication may, therefore, initiate a 
self-amplifying sequence of maladaptive responses, including extra-
cellular matrix (ECM) remodelling, myelin disruption and deficits 
in cognition.
The convergence hypothesis offers an alternative view, arguing 
that overnutrition simultaneously disrupts multiple systems, each of 
which contributes independently to neurodegeneration. Yet given the 
extensive cross-talk among NVU components, few processes operate 
in true isolation. Perturbations in vascular signalling rapidly influence 
glial states; inflammatory activation alters barrier function; metabolic 
stress reshapes neuronal firing. Thus, what appears as parallel pathol-
ogy at the tissue level may, at the cellular level, represent a deeply 
interconnected system undergoing progressive decoupling.
We propose a unified ‘convergent-cascade’ framework that recon-
ciles both models. Early metabolic overload produces a set of parallel 
disruptions (altered NVC, reduced CBF, impaired fluid clearance) that 
arise concurrently and may initially be compensatory. Over time, accu-
mulated dysfunction reaches a tipping point (‘convergence’), after 
which vascular stress, glial activation and barrier breakdown begin to 
amplify each other in a non-linear manner49. This escalation produces 
a downstream ‘cascade’ of maladaptive outcomes, including ECM 
destabilization, myelin loss and network-level impairment (Fig. 1). This 
model better reflects the multifactorial nature of obesity and aligns 
with analogous debates in metabolic biology, such as carbohydrate–
insulin versus energy-balance models, where overlapping mechanisms 
reinforce one another over time.
In the next sections, we dissect how metabolic overload per-
turbs individual components of brain homeostasis and then inte-
grate these findings into a cohesive systems-level framework of 
obesity-related neurodegeneration.
Disrupted neurovascular communication in 
obesity
NVC has a central role in maintaining cerebral homeostasis. It matches 
local neuronal demand with appropriate nutrient delivery and supports 
waste-clearance mechanisms50. Disruption of this tightly regulated 
mechanism has been observed across neurological disorders and is 
frequently accompanied by alterations in CBF51. Chronic overnutri-
tion predictably perturbs both NVC and CBF, probably reflecting an 
initial adaptive response to sustained metabolic excess that becomes 
maladaptive over time52,53.
Human studies consistently demonstrate that higher body mass 
index (BMI) is associated with global reductions in CBF and dimin-
ished cerebrovascular reactivity, particularly in regions implicated 
in Alzheimer’s disease pathology, suggesting a mechanistic pathway

Physical activity exerts protective effects on cerebrovascular 
function in individuals with obesity58. Notably, neurovascular altera-
tions also emerge during adolescence, where obesity is associated with 
reduced CBF in the cerebellum, precentral gyrus and supplementary 
motor area60. Similar reductions are seen in individuals with T2DM, 
particularly within the hippocampus, occipital and parietal cortices, 
and amygdala61,62. These data point to a global shift in brain haemody-
namics in response to metabolic shifts.
Converging evidence from animal models strengthens this inter-
pretation. In HFD-fed mice, overnutrition impairs sensory-evoked CBF 
in the somatosensory cortex and decreases resting and odour-evoked 
CBF in the olfactory bulb63,64. Given the olfactory bulb’s extensive 
projections to emotion- and memory-related circuits65, olfactory loss 
is a known early marker of neurological decline, and vascular dysfunc-
tion in this region may represent an early sentinel of broader neural 
vulnerability66. The hippocampus is similarly affected: a HFD reduces 
hippocampal CBF in mice, which is associated with both visceral adi-
posity and cognitive function67, and early T2DM impairs hippocampal 
NVC in rats via reduced nitric oxide bioavailability68. These findings 
reinforce altered haemodynamic signalling as a potential early driver 
of metabolically induced neurodegeneration.
Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that neuro-
vascular uncoupling and reduced CBF may serve as initiating events 
in obesity-induced neurodegeneration (Fig. 2). The brain may, at 
least transiently, attempt to compensate via structural remod-
elling of vascular architecture, a response with both adaptive and 
pathological implications69,70.
Structural reorganization of the cerebral 
vasculature
As the effector arm of NVC, the cerebral vasculature dynamically adjusts 
blood flow to match local metabolic demand71. This network, com-
posed of endothelial cells, pericytes, smooth muscle cells and astrocyte 
endfeet, continuously interprets metabolic and inflammatory signals 
to maintain homeostasis. In obesity and neurodegenerative disease, 
however, this system undergoes profound remodelling, ranging from 
hypovascularization in vulnerable cortical regions to compensatory 
hypervascularization in metabolically active areas72.
Pathological angiogenesis is a well-recognized feature of neuro-
degeneration. In Alzheimer’s disease, both amyloid-β and tau induce 
aberrant vascular growth: amyloid-β promotes angiogenesis and dis-
rupts the BBB, whereas tau drives vessel narrowing, abnormal spiralling 
morphologies and increased but dysfunctional vessel density73. These 
vessels often fail to deliver adequate nutrients and oxygen, creating 
microdomains of hypoxia that destabilize the NVU and amplify both 
neuronal and vascular injury. Such maladaptive angiogenesis reflects 
a breakdown in normal coupling between vascular remodelling and 
metabolic demand.
Obesity appears to engage similar pathways. In humans, BMI cor-
relates positively with tortuosity of small cerebral arteries, a marker of 
pathological modelling associated with nearby white matter lesions74. 
Post-mortem studies show increased arteriolar density in the hypo-
thalamus of individuals with T2DM, consistent with region-specific 
angiogenesis75. In murine models, a HFD or a high-fat high-sucrose 
(HFHS) diet induces increased vessel length and density, along with

Regional heterogeneity in remodelling suggests a broader organ-
izing principle: brain areas at the brain–body interface (for example, 
hypothalamus and circumventricular organs) exhibit hypervascu-
larization to enhance nutrient sensing, whereas higher-order cogni-
tive regions, which lack such adaptive remodelling, become more 
susceptible to hypoperfusion and vulnerability. Irrespective of direc-
tion, sustained vascular dysfunction is closely linked to increased BBB 
permeability, immune activation and neuroinflammation80.
BBB breakdown and immune cell infiltration
The BBB serves as both a protective filter and a communication inter-
face between the brain and periphery81. BBB dysfunction is a hallmark 
of ageing and neurodegeneration, and growing evidence indicates that 
it has a central role in obesity-induced brain vulnerability82. In women 
with overweight or obesity, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-to-serum albu-
min ratio, an index of BBB permeability, is higher than in lean control 
women83. Individuals with T2DM show similarly elevated BBB perme-
ability to magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents84, with serum 
albumin ratio positively correlating with CSF biomarkers of endothelial 
dysfunction85. Post-mortem analysis reveals loss of tight-junction pro-
teins and increased immune cell infiltration in T2DM brains, indicating 
widespread vascular leakage86.
Interestingly, leptin transport across the BBB is impaired in obe-
sity87. Although this might look paradoxical at first, increased passive 
permeability to inert tracers and reduced active receptor-mediated 
transport of hormones are consistent with a compromised BBB. 
Chronic vascular stress thus creates a permissive environment 
for neurodegeneration.
The hippocampus is the region where BBB breakdown most con-
sistently tracks with cognitive decline88. Accumulating research sug-
gests that the hippocampal BBB is more vulnerable to breakdown 
compared to other brain regions89. Although studies examining hip-
pocampal BBB integrity in humans with obesity are limited, preclinical 
studies indicate BBB dysfunction in obesity. In mice, 12–16 weeks of 
high-fat feeding result in increased tracer penetration and reduced 
tight-junction protein expression in hippocampus, whereas no abnor-
malities are detectable at 8 weeks90. Remarkably, measurable increases 
in BBB permeability emerge within 1 h of high-fat exposure91, and 3 
days of feeding induce permeability changes associated with learning 
and memory deficits92. These findings suggest a temporal sequence: 
(1) immediate functional permeability changes (hours–days), (2) 
compensatory stabilization period (weeks) and, (3) structural BBB 
breakdown (months).
A landmark study by Jais et al.93 revealed a mechanistic basis for this 
progression. Short-term HFD feeding (3 days) downregulates GLUT1 
expression in BBB endothelial cells, reducing glucose transport into the 
brain as a compensatory mechanism93. Prolonged exposure (28 days) 
restores GLUT1 levels in conjunction with increased vascular endothe-
lial growth factor (VEGF) expression in BBB-associated macrophages, 
suggesting an attempt to remodel the vasculature in response to nutri-
ent stress. In chronic HFD conditions, myeloid-specific VEGF deletion 
further increases BBB permeability and decreases GLUT1 expression 
in the cortex and hippocampus. These findings point to a model in 
which both hypervascularization and hypovascularization contribute 
to a leaky BBB, depending on regional vascular capacity and meta-
bolic demand94. Notably, hypothalamic regions, designed for dynamic 
nutrient sensing, may be more capable of compensatory vascular 
adaptation95. By contrast, cortex and hippocampus, regions with more 
stable vascular structures, are less plastic and, therefore, more prone 
to dysfunction. This regional disparity may explain why certain brain 
areas are more vulnerable to obesity-induced neurodegeneration.
BBB breakdown permits the infiltration of peripheral immune 
cells, further altering the neuroimmune landscape96. In mice, one 
week of a HFHS diet leads to infiltration of macrophages into the hypo-
thalamus97. Whereas changes in regulatory T cells occur more gradu-
ally, their decline over time correlates with increased type 1 helper 
T cells, shifting the immune balance towards chronic inflammation. 
Gain-of-function and loss-of-function studies confirm that regulatory 
T cells modulate hypothalamic inflammation and metabolic health97. 
Similar infiltration occurs in cortex and septum98, and correlates 
strongly with body weight and adipose inflammation.
These findings highlight a neuroinflammatory response to 
primary metabolic overload in the brain as an initial compensatory 
mechanism that becomes a chronic, secondary maladaptive response 
to metabolic dysfunction (Fig. 3). In summary, obesity-induced neu-
roinflammation is best understood as a two-phase process: an early, 
localized hypothalamic response to nutrient excess, followed by a 
secondary amplification driven by peripheral metabolic dysfunction 
and systemic inflammation.
This escalating immune system activation triggers downstream 
processes, including the release of matrix metalloproteases that 
degrade components of the ECM, setting the stage for structural dis-
integration and further functional decline99.
ECM destabilization and PNN vulnerability
Once considered a passive structural scaffold, the ECM is now rec-
ognized as a dynamic regulator of the neural microenvironment, 
modulating synaptic plasticity, neuronal excitability and resistance 
to oxidative and mechanical stress100. ECM dysfunction is increasingly 
implicated across neurological diseases, yet its role in metabolic over-
load is only beginning to be appreciated101. Emerging evidence suggests 
that endothelial cell remodelling may represent a critical mechanism 
linking obesity-induced inflammation to altered neural circuit function.
In the hypothalamus of obese mice, perineuronal nets (PNNs), 
specialized ECM structures surrounding neuronal soma, undergo 
augmentation and restructuring, a phenomenon recently described 
as neurofibrosis102. This remodelling is primarily driven by hypotha-
lamic inflammation, which precedes and predicts metabolic dysfunc-
tion. Notably, PNN changes are concentrated around agouti-related 
peptide neurons in the arcuate nucleus, increasing their excitability 
and promoting hyperphagia102. These neurofibrotic changes not only 
enhance food intake but also impede insulin access to target neurons, 
contributing to central insulin resistance. Disruption of these aber-
rantly stabilized PNNs restores metabolic homeostasis and promotes 
weight loss, identifying ECM remodelling as a promising therapeutic 
target for metabolic diseases.
However, the relationship is not uniform. PNNs in the median 
eminence are required for maintaining energy balance in lean mice103. 
This suggests that chronic metabolic overload may drive pathological 
amplification of PNNs, parallelling the maladaptive vascular remodel-
ling seen in obesity. However, the role of obesity in regulation of hypo-
thalamic PNNs remains unsettled as other studies in HFD-fed mice have 
found no increase in PNN formation in the arcuate nucleus104. These 
discrepancies highlight methodological and temporal differences that 
require further clarification.

pal circuits show distinct vulnerabilities. Short-term HFD or HFHS

while hypothalamic circuits exhibit neurofibrotic PNN amplifica-

regions show PNN degradation or pathological accumulation, 
depending on exposure duration, contributing to cognitive impair-

interstitial space, its destabilization may also impair glymphatic 
clearance, promoting accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites 
such as amyloid-β and tau109,110. This positions ECM remodelling 
as a central mediator in the bridge between metabolic overload 
and neurodegeneration.
Impaired glymphatic flow and accumulation of 
neurotoxic metabolites
The discovery of the glymphatic system reframed CSF flow as an active, 
regulated waste-clearance pathway essential for solute exchange and 
metabolic homeostasis111. Glymphatic efficiency depends on sleep 
architecture, circadian rhythms, vascular pulsatility and astrocytic 
aquaporin 4 (AQP4) polarization, all of which are disrupted in obesity112. 
These vulnerabilities make glymphatic function particularly sensitive 
to metabolic overload.

Human imaging studies link higher BMI to enlarged perivascular 
spaces and altered vasomotor oscillations across cerebellar, brainstem, 
insular and parietal regions113, patterns indicative of impaired intersti-
tial waste clearance114. Glymphatic dysfunction is increasingly reported 
across metabolic and neurodegenerative conditions: in Parkinson’s dis-
ease, higher BMI associates with reduced glymphatic function115,116, and 
obesity-related idiopathic intracranial hypertension features increased 
extraventricular CSF volume and reduced venous outflow117. Parallel 
discoveries in meningeal lymphatics, including lymphangiogenesis 
near the cribriform plate and antigen sampling in dural lymphoid 
structures, underscore an active immunological interface that may 
be especially vulnerable to metabolic stress118,119.
A seminal study by Delle et al.120 demonstrated region-specific 
glymphatic remodelling in response to long-term HFD feeding. The 
hypothalamus showed increased glymphatic influx and enhanced 
AQP4 polarization, features that mirror its elevated vascularization 
and BBB permeability, and may represent an adaptive response to 
heightened metabolic demand. By contrast, glymphatic clearance and 
AQP4 polarization were reduced in cortex and hippocampus, aligning 
with their well-established vulnerability to cognitive decline121. Similar 
reductions in AQP4 levels across frontal–striatal circuits of obese mice, 
accompanied by gliosis and demyelination122, reinforce the interde-
pendence of vascular, glial and fluid-transport pathways.
Animal models of T2DM further support these findings: hip-
pocampal glymphatic flow is reduced in T2DM rats and correlates with 
memory impairments123. Although the relationship between specific 
metabolic phenotypes and glymphatic failure remains incompletely 
defined, the overarching principle is clear: altered metabolic states 
reshape brain-wide waste-clearance capacity116.
These findings align with a broader pattern: nutrient-sensing 
regions such as the hypothalamus can augment vascular and glym-
phatic function under nutrient excess. By contrast, areas with limited 
exposure to circulating signals (cognitive loci) lack this flexibility and 
may be predisposed to dysfunction under metabolic duress. Dys-
regulated glymphatic flow promotes accumulation of amyloid-β, 
tau and other neurotoxic metabolites, accelerating white matter 
damage and cognitive decline124. Thus, glymphatic impairment 
represents a key mechanistic link between metabolic overload and 
progressive neurodegeneration.

initiating cognitive decline rather than merely reflecting it126. Obesity 
increases the risk of neurological disorders well before clinical symp-

In cognitively unimpaired adults, increased BMI and central adi-

within later-myelinating tracts such as the corona radiata, longitudinal 
fasciculus and thalamic radiations128. These regions are highly sus-

hypothesis, which posits that circuits myelinated late in development 
degenerate first under metabolic stress. Composite metabolic risk 
scores also predict lower myelin density in late-developing tracts129. Vis-
ceral obesity is associated with increased white matter hyperintensities 
in the deep white matter, an association that persists independently of 
common obesity comorbidities such as hypertension130. These micro-
structural changes correlate with reduced cognitive performance131.
Preclinical work begins to reveal underlying mechanisms. In mice, 
chronic HFD feeding induces hypothalamic demyelination, persis-
tent microglial activation and elevated inflammatory cytokines132. 
These white matter abnormalities appear later than early immune 
and vascular changes, suggesting that demyelination may represent 
a downstream but integral phase of neurometabolic decline. Nota-
bly, reduced myelin content also occurs in the corpus callosum, fron-
tal cortex and hippocampus, regions associated with cognition and 
interhemispheric communication133.
Despite increased numbers of immature oligodendrocytes and 
upregulated myelin-related genes, these regenerative responses 
fail to restore structural integrity, suggesting a maladaptive or 
exhausted repair programme. Genetic models reinforce this princi-
ple: leptin-deficient ob/ob mice show profound hypomyelination and 
cognitive deficits134, driven by chronic inflammation and the loss of 
leptin’s pro-myelinating actions135. Similar processes probably occur 
in diet-induced obesity, where leptin resistance compromises oligo-
dendrocyte maturation and white matter stability.
In aged mice, visceral adiposity is associated with iron deposition 
and demyelination, supporting systemic inflammation as a key factor for 
driving neurodegeneration136. Surgical removal of visceral fat reduces 
central iron levels and ameliorates myelin loss in the cortex and corpus 
callosum. Importantly, voluntary running, independent of weight loss, 
reverses many of the obesity-induced structural abnormalities in these 
regions, including demyelination and cerebrovascular dysfunction133, 
underscoring the potential of behavioural interventions to restore white 
matter integrity even under persistent metabolic stress.
Taken together, these findings position white matter degradation 
as both a consequence and contributor to neurometabolic decline. In 
contrast to earlier, regionally heterogeneous changes, demyelination 
appears to propagate more uniformly across the brain, indicating that 
any sustained disruption to NVU function, whether through impaired 
glymphatic flow, vascular remodelling or immune infiltration, can 
compromise oligodendrocyte health and long-range network con-
nectivity (Fig. 4).
Reframing neurodegeneration through a brain–
body framework
Addressing the complexity of disorders such as obesity and neuro-
degeneration demands interdisciplinary perspectives. The emerg-
ing brain–body field reflects a transdisciplinary approach aimed at 
understanding the bidirectional interactions between central and 
peripheral systems137. This framework has reshaped our understand-
ing of metabolic and neurological disease by demonstrating that they 
share overlapping biological substrates and reciprocal regulatory 
loops, coining the ‘neurometabolic’ field.
A seminal example is the discovery of the hormone leptin, which 
catalysed a conceptual shift by revealing extensive brain–body 
cross-talk between adipose tissue and the brain138. Leptin transformed 
obesity from a peripheral disorder of adiposity into a systemic condi-
tion involving long-range neural, endocrine and immunological cir-
cuits. Insights into the neurobiology of feeding behaviour and energy 
balance subsequently led to the development of glucagon-like peptide 
1 receptor (GLP1R) agonists, which have revolutionized clinical manage-
ment of obesity and T2DM139. This same conceptual lens, identifying 
systemic metabolic drivers of neural dysfunction, is now increasingly 
applied to neurodegeneration. Preliminary data suggest that metabolic 
interventions such as GLP1R agonists and metformin may complement 
traditional neurological therapies140,141, although whether their benefits 
arise from peripheral metabolic improvement, direct central nervous 
system actions or both remains an active area of investigation (Box 1).
The overarching message is integration. A neuron-centric view of 
neurodegeneration is no longer sufficient; instead, the full repertoire 
of central nervous system cell types (neurons, astrocytes, microglia, 
oligodendrocytes and endothelial cells) must be incorporated into 
models of disease. Similarly, rigid distinctions between ‘central’ and

Repurposing metabolic drugs for brain–body neurodegeneration
The purpose of viewing neurological disorders through a brain–body 
framework is to tackle the multifaceted nature of these complex 
pathologies. Owing to the interconnectedness between metabolic 
and neurological disorders, it is within reason to suggest that 
traditional metabolic drugs can be utilized for neurodegeneration. By 
preventing or treating metabolic dysfunctions with these metabolic 
drugs, they are indirectly mitigating the risk of neurological disorders. 
These drugs also demonstrate direct effects on the brain by 
targeting a variety of cell types and processes53. In individuals with 
mild-to-moderate Alzheimer’s disease, treatment with the GLP1R 
agonist liraglutide for 12 months reduces brain atrophy and cognition 
compared to the placebo group142. Similarly, the use of dipeptidyl 
peptidase 4 inhibitors reduces the rate of memory decline in people 
with Alzheimer’s disease, whereas metformin improves memory 
performance in healthy control individuals143. Treatment with GLP1R 
agonists or sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors decreases the 
risk of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias in patients with 
T2DM144.
In aged mice, metformin induces remyelination by restoring the 
regenerative capacity of aged oligodendrocytes145. Linagliptin, a 
dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitor, ameliorates cognitive impairment 
and brain insulin resistance in a rat model of Alzheimer’s disease146. 
A combination of canonical neurodegenerative therapies with 
complementary metabolic interventions may enhance treatment 
efficacy. For example, co-administration of metformin and donepezil 
improves cognitive function and abnormal glucose metabolism in 
individuals with non-dementia vascular cognitive impairment147. 
The same combination improves brain antioxidant defences and 
attenuates hippocampal endoplasmic reticulum stress in diabetic 
rats148. Although the data on human clinical trials are mixed, 
preclinical models demonstrate the potential of these metabolic 
drugs to improve neurological outcomes149. Future optimization of 
the dose, timing and duration of treatment is required to maximize 
the therapeutic efficacy. It is also important to determine the optimal 
treatment window by identifying the proper patient populations that 
will most likely benefit from these treatments.

‘peripheral’ disorders obscure the multifactorial nature of pathologi-

classified as a peripheral disorder, illustrates this principle: chronic 
metabolic overload reshapes neurovascular, immune and glial systems, 
thereby elevating long-term neurological risk.
A holistic brain–body framework opens avenues for interven-
tion by acknowledging multiple interacting nodes of vulnerability 
(metabolic, vascular, inflammatory, synaptic and structural) that can 
be targeted to break self-reinforcing loops of degeneration.
Conclusion
Once regarded as separate clinical entities, obesity and neurologi-
cal disorders are now understood to share deeply intertwined patho-
physiological mechanisms. The obese brain exhibits features that 
map onto multiple models of neurodegenerative vulnerability: con-
vergent initiation, cascade propagation, tipping-point transitions, 
network-level failure and, ultimately, multisystem homeostatic col-
lapse. Chronic metabolic overload acts as a systemic stressor that per-
turbs neurons, glia, vasculature and immune cells. These disruptions 
are not isolated; instead, they interact dynamically across space and 
time, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of vulnerability. Importantly, 
metabolic stress increases the probability, not the inevitability, of 
neurodegenerative outcomes.
By integrating cascade and convergence models into a unified 
framework, we highlight where these processes intersect and how they 
evolve. This systems-level view reveals critical windows for interven-
tion: blocking early triggers, slowing progressive cascades or inter-
rupting feedback loops that amplify dysfunction.
Addressing the neurometabolic consequences of obesity will 
require strategies that extend beyond weight loss to target the cellular 
and molecular underpinnings of brain vulnerability. The fact that the 
system collapses only under multiple insults suggests that it may also 
be rescued through multiple therapeutic entry points. By elucidating 
the complex brain–body interactions that govern neurometabolic 
health, we can lay the foundation for more effective treatments for 
both metabolic and neurological diseases and ultimately bridge the 
gap between them.
