    Shared neural basis psychiatric comorbidity

    Nature Medicine | Volume 29 | May 2023 | 1232–1242 Article https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-023-02317-4

    A shared neural basis underlying psychiatric comorbidity

    Chao Xie1,2, Shitong Xiang1,2, Chun Shen1,2, Xuerui Peng3, Jujiao Kang1,2, Yuzhu Li1,2, Wei Cheng1,2, Shiqi He1,4, Marina Bobou5, M. John Broulidakis6, Betteke Maria van Noort7, Zuo Zhang5, Lauren Robinson8,9, Nilakshi Vaidya10, Jeanne Winterer10,11, Yuning Zhang12, Sinead King5,13, Tobias Banaschewski14, Gareth J. Barker15, Arun L. W. Bokde16, Uli Bromberg17, Christian Büchel17, Herta Flor18,19, Antoine Grigis20, Hugh Garavan21, Penny Gowland22, Andreas Heinz10, Bernd Ittermann23, Hervé Lemaître24, Jean-Luc Martinot25, Marie-Laure Paillère Martinot25,26, Frauke Nees14,18,27, Dimitri Papadopoulos Orfanos20, Tomáš Paus28, Luise Poustka29, Juliane H. Fröhner30, Ulrike Schmidt8,9, Julia Sinclair6, Michael N. Smolka30, Argyris Stringaris31, Henrik Walter10, Robert Whelan32, Sylvane Desrivières5, Barbara J. Sahakian1,33, Trevor W. Robbins1,34,40, Gunter Schumann1,10,35,36,40, Tianye Jia1,2,5,40, Jianfeng Feng1,2,37,38,39,40, IMAGEN Consortium, STRATIFY/ESTRA Consortium & ZIB Consortium*

    Recent studies proposed a general psychopathology factor underlying common comorbidities among psychiatric disorders. However, its neurobiological mechanisms and generalizability remain elusive. In this study, we used a large longitudinal neuroimaging cohort from adolescence to young adulthood (IMAGEN) to define a neuropsychopathological (NP) factor across externalizing and internalizing symptoms using multitask connectomes. We demonstrate that this NP factor might represent a unified, genetically determined, delayed development of the prefrontal cortex that further leads to poor executive function. We also show this NP factor to be reproducible in multiple developmental periods, from preadolescence to early adulthood, and generalizable to the resting-state connectome and clinical samples (the ADHD-200 Sample and the STRATIFY & ESTRA Project). In conclusion, we identify a reproducible and general neural basis underlying symptoms of multiple mental health disorders, bridging multidimensional evidence from behavioral, neuroimaging and genetic substrates. These findings may help to develop new therapeutic interventions for psychiatric comorbidities.

    The coexistence of multiple psychiatric conditions, known as psychiatric comorbidity1, has garnered substantial attention due to its high prevalence and long-lasting impact2. Individuals with comorbid psychiatric diagnoses often experience poorer outcomes and severe deficits in various cognitive and behavioral domains3. Notably, many psychiatric disorders, for example, externalizing and internalizing disorders, have their approximate peak onset in adolescence, coinciding with the emergence of comorbidity4,5. For instance, a population-based study on the well-being of adolescents found that 27.9% of participants aged 14–17 reached multiple diagnostic criteria6. The high prevalence of comorbid mental disorders suggests shared neurobiological origins among different psychopathologies2. However, the neuropsychopathological mechanism of psychiatric comorbidity, particularly during the critical period of adolescence, remains elusive.

    Recently, emerging evidence has suggested a general psychopathology factor (that is, the p factor) underlying higher vulnerability for different psychiatric disorders7. Statistically, the p factor summarizes a pattern of positive correlations among symptoms; however, it leaves no room for alternative latent effects (for example, anticorrelation among symptoms). Indeed, it was argued that the behavioral p factor is largely equivalent to a sum of all symptoms8. Further, previous neuroimaging studies that investigated the neural correlates of the p factor mainly relied on task-free modalities, such as resting state9, diffusion10 and structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)11. However, although these task-free neural correlates of this p factor represent varied neurobiological information, they do not aid in specifying the neurocognitive processes underlying multiple psychopathologies12,13. Instead, the relevant cognitive brain circuitry can be mapped using multitask functional MRI (fMRI) data, which have also been used previously to identify circuit-specific neural signatures of externalizing symptoms14.

    In contrast, crossdisorder genetic studies further revealed that many psychiatric disorders share high degrees of positive genetic correlations15,16, and the common genetic variants predominantly involved neurodevelopmental processes17,18. However, opposite genetic effects were also identified among psychiatric disorders17, which further highlighted the complexity of shared biological processes across multiple mental disorders. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate behavioral, neuroimaging and genetic evidence to establish coherent neurobiological crossdisorder neural factors (that is, the NP factor) that are not only shared among different psychopathologies but could also be attributed to specific cognitive brain circuits and genetic variants.

    Notably, mounting evidence propose that many mental disorders can be understood as extreme deviations from a continuous spectrum in the population and different mental disorders may demonstrate similar deficits in multiple cognitive functions, as envisaged by Research Domain Criteria19. This new understanding inspires us to investigate potential transdiagnostic neurobiological processes from population-based data that are enriched with task-based fMRIs of multiple cognitive domains and symptom measurements covering a wide range of mental disorders. This approach also allows us to avoid the dilemma of case–control studies in identifying transdiagnostic biomarkers, where comorbidity is generally considered a major confounding factor to be removed. Furthermore, considering the replication crisis in neuroimaging studies, it was suggested that combining large neuroimaging samples and machine-learning approaches (that is, combining training and validation processes) could increase the reliability and reproducibility of identified neurobiomarkers20,21.

    In this study, we will address the following three specific major questions regarding the shared neural bases of behavioral symptoms related to psychiatric disorders (Fig. 1a): (1) Can we establish an NP factor underlying both externalizing and internalizing symptoms on the basis of the multiple task-based connectomes of fMRI? (2) Is the NP factor supported by genetic and neurobehavioral substrates (for example, polygenic risk scores (PRSs), single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and cognitive functions)? (3) Is the NP factor generalizable to different imaging modalities (for example, resting-state connectome), developmental periods and clinical samples?

    Results

    Summary of major analytic steps

    First, we leveraged the population-based IMAGEN cohort (aged 14 years, N = 1,750, 882 girls; Extended Data Table 1) to estimate the brain signature of eight behavioral symptoms using the connectivity-based predictive model grounded on multiple task-based fMRIs (Fig. 1b). These symptoms included four externalizing symptoms (that is, ASD, ADHD, ODD and CD) and four internalizing symptoms (that is, GAD, depression, ED and SP). The multitask connectomes were constructed from three fMRI tasks: stop-signal task (SST; related to response inhibition), monetary incentive delay task (MID; related to reward processing) and emotional face task (EFT; related to emotion reactivity). The model used 11 training–validation pairs to increase reliability.

    We then extracted shared edges across the eight symptom-specific brain signatures to identify potential crossdisorder brain signatures. Using reliability and longitudinal analyses, we identified the NP factor that was positively predictive for both externalizing and internalizing symptoms across ages 14 and 19 (Fig. 1b–c). This NP factor was characterized by functional connectivity (FC) strength within three networks: sensorimotor network (SMF), salience network (SAL) and frontoparietal network (FPN), involving regions such as ventral precuneus (vPCun), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and inferior frontal gyrus (IFG).

    After identifying the NP factor at age 14, we examined its neurobehavioral and genetic underpinnings. We found that the NP factor was associated with poor executive function (Fig. 2a), including lower IQ, reduced working memory capacity and impaired risk adjustment ability. Furthermore, the NP factor was genetically associated with ADHD and depression PRSs (Fig. 2b) and linked to neurodevelopmental SNPs (Fig. 2c). The genetic associations were specific to neurodevelopmental processes rather than other pathways.

    To test generalizability, we validated the NP factor in multiple contexts: (1) different developmental periods (ages 10–11, 14, 19, 23 and 29) across ABCD, IMAGEN and HCP cohorts; (2) resting-state connectomes; and (3) clinical samples including ADHD-200 (N = 520) and STRATIFY/ESTRA (N = 413, covering AN, BN, AUD and MDD). The NP factor showed consistent predictive power for symptom scores across all these contexts (Fig. 3), indicating its robustness and generalizability.

    Establishing the NP factor using multitask connectomes

    We first constructed eight connectome-based predictive models (CPMs) for each of the eight behavioral symptoms using the multitask connectomes at age 14 in the IMAGEN cohort. Each CPM included 11 independent training–validation pairs to increase reliability. The models achieved significant prediction accuracy for most symptoms (r = 0.12–0.22, P < 0.001 for all symptoms except ED).

    We then identified edges that were significantly positively or negatively correlated with each symptom across all 11 folds. The number of symptom-specific edges varied: ASD (2,409 positive, 1,945 negative), ADHD (2,738 positive, 2,246 negative), ODD (2,108 positive, 2,149 negative), CD (2,001 positive, 2,012 negative), GAD (1,894 positive, 2,007 negative), depression (1,798 positive, 1,998 negative), ED (1,654 positive, 1,698 negative), SP (1,887 positive, 2,009 negative).

    To identify crossdisorder edges, we calculated the proportion of edges shared between each pair of symptoms. Externalizing symptoms (ASD, ADHD, ODD, CD) showed high overlap (59–78%), while internalizing symptoms (GAD, depression, ED, SP) showed moderate overlap (44–67%). Cross-group overlap between externalizing and internalizing symptoms was lower but still substantial (20–37%).

    We extracted the shared edges from all eight symptoms, resulting in 1,246 positive edges (associated with higher symptoms) and 1,609 negative edges (associated with lower symptoms). These edges formed the basis of the NP factor.

    Reliability and longitudinal analysis of the NP factor

    We calculated the NP factor score for each participant by summing the FC strength of all shared edges. The NP factor score was significantly associated with total externalizing symptoms (r = 0.30, P < 0.001) and total internalizing symptoms (r = 0.24, P < 0.001) at age 14, indicating its predictive power for both symptom dimensions.

    To test reliability, we computed the NP factor score using each of the 11 training–validation folds and examined test–retest reliability across folds. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.94, indicating excellent reliability.

    To test longitudinal stability, we recalculated the NP factor score using the same edges but at age 19 (5 years later). The NP factor score at age 19 was significantly correlated with scores at age 14 (r = 0.57, P < 0.001), demonstrating temporal stability. The NP factor at age 19 also predicted externalizing symptoms (r = 0.24, P < 0.001) and internalizing symptoms (r = 0.19, P < 0.001), though the correlation was slightly weaker than at age 14.

    Characterization of the NP factor

    Network-level analysis revealed that the NP factor primarily involved three networks: SMF, SAL and FPN. The positive edges (associated with higher symptoms) showed weaker FC within these networks, suggesting that reduced connectivity in these networks is associated with higher symptom severity. The negative edges (associated with lower symptoms) showed stronger FC between these networks and other regions, indicating that enhanced connectivity between these networks and other brain regions is protective.

    Region-level analysis identified three key regions: vPCun, mPFC and IFG. These regions were hubs in the NP factor, with high centrality in both positive and negative edge networks.

    Neurobehavioral characterization showed that higher NP factor scores (indicating weaker connectivity) were associated with poorer executive function. Specifically, individuals with higher NP factor scores had lower IQ (r = -0.18, P < 0.001), reduced working memory capacity (r = -0.15, P < 0.001) and impaired risk adjustment ability (r = -0.12, P < 0.001). In contrast, the NP factor was not significantly associated with non-executive cognitive domains such as processing speed or memory consolidation.

    Genetic characterization revealed that the NP factor was genetically associated with ADHD PRS (r = -0.10, P = 0.002) and depression PRS (r = -0.09, P = 0.004). Higher genetic risk for ADHD and depression was associated with weaker connectivity in the NP factor. Gene ontology analysis of SNPs associated with the NP factor showed enrichment for neurodevelopmental processes (P < 0.001), including neuronal differentiation, axon guidance and synapse formation. No enrichment was found for immune or metabolic pathways.

    Generalizability of the NP factor

    We tested the NP factor in multiple independent cohorts and contexts. First, we examined developmental generalizability by computing NP factor scores in different age groups across three cohorts: ABCD (ages 10–11, N = 1,946), IMAGEN (age 14, N = 931; age 19, N = 653) and HCP (age 29, N = 1,081). The NP factor score consistently predicted both externalizing and internalizing symptoms across all age groups (r = 0.15–0.35, P < 0.01 for all associations), indicating that the NP factor is stable across development.

    Second, we examined modality generalizability by computing NP factor scores using resting-state connectomes instead of task-based connectomes. In the IMAGEN cohort, the resting-state NP factor score predicted externalizing symptoms (r = 0.22, P < 0.001) and internalizing symptoms (r = 0.18, P < 0.001), confirming that the NP factor generalizes to resting-state data.

    Third, we examined clinical generalizability by testing the NP factor in two clinical samples: ADHD-200 (N = 520, age range 7–21) and STRATIFY/ESTRA (N = 413, age range 16–27, covering AN, BN, AUD and MDD). In ADHD-200, the NP factor score predicted ADHD symptom severity (r = 0.28, P < 0.001). In STRATIFY/ESTRA, the NP factor score predicted symptoms across all four disorders (AN: r = 0.19, P = 0.003; BN: r = 0.21, P = 0.001; AUD: r = 0.24, P < 0.001; MDD: r = 0.26, P < 0.001). These results indicate that the NP factor generalizes to clinical populations.

    Developmental trajectory of the NP factor

    To examine the developmental trajectory of the NP factor, we compared NP factor scores across ages using the IMAGEN and ABCD cohorts. We found that NP factor scores decreased with age, indicating that the connectivity patterns associated with psychopathology normalize during development. The reduction was most pronounced between ages 14 and 19 (mean decrease: 0.45 SD), suggesting that adolescence is a critical period for the maturation of these networks.

    We also examined whether NP factor scores at age 14 predicted symptom trajectories from age 14 to 19. Higher NP factor scores at age 14 predicted slower reduction in externalizing symptoms (β = 0.32, P < 0.001) and internalizing symptoms (β = 0.28, P < 0.001), indicating that the NP factor predicts persistence of symptoms.

    Discussion

    In this study, using a large longitudinal neuroimaging genetic cohort, we identified a reliable neural endophenotype (that is, the NP factor) of behavioral symptoms for multiple mental disorders, with implications for early prevention and therapeutics in psychiatry.

    We constructed the crossdisorder brain signature through the intersection of symptom-specific brain signatures derived from multitask connectomes in a population-based sample. This NP factor was validated across multiple developmental periods, imaging modalities and clinical samples. Its neurobehavioral and genetic underpinnings were characterized, revealing associations with executive function deficits and neurodevelopmental genetic variants.

    Our findings advance the understanding of psychiatric comorbidity in several ways. First, we identified a transdiagnostic neural factor that underlies both externalizing and internalizing symptoms, bridging the internalizing–externalizing divide that has dominated psychiatric nosology22. Second, we demonstrated that this NP factor is reliable and stable across development, supporting its use as a biomarker for early identification of individuals at risk for multiple mental disorders. Third, we showed that the NP factor generalizes to clinical samples, indicating its potential utility in clinical settings. Fourth, we characterized the neurobehavioral and genetic underpinnings of the NP factor, providing mechanistic insights into how shared neurobiological factors contribute to psychiatric comorbidity.

    The NP factor primarily involved three networks: SMF, SAL and FPN. These networks are critical for executive function, cognitive control and salience processing23–25. Reduced connectivity within these networks was associated with higher symptom severity, suggesting that impaired executive control and salience processing contribute to psychopathology. This interpretation is supported by our neurobehavioral findings, which showed that higher NP factor scores were associated with poorer executive function but not with non-executive cognitive domains.

    The involvement of SMF in the NP factor is notable, as this network has traditionally been associated with motor and sensory processing rather than higher cognitive functions. However, recent evidence suggests that SMF plays a role in sensorimotor integration, action planning and cognitive control26,27. Our findings align with this emerging perspective and highlight the importance of considering sensorimotor processes in psychopathology.

    The involvement of SAL and FPN is more consistent with prior literature. The SAL network is involved in detecting behaviorally relevant stimuli and switching between default mode and executive control networks28,29. The FPN is involved in working memory, decision-making and cognitive control30,31. Dysfunction in these networks has been implicated in various psychiatric disorders32,33. Our findings extend this literature by showing that dysfunction in these networks is shared across disorders and contributes to comorbidity.

    The key regions identified (vPCun, mPFC and IFG) are also consistent with prior literature. The vPCun is part of the default mode network and is involved in self-referential processing and episodic memory retrieval34,35. The mPFC is involved in emotion regulation, decision-making and social cognition36,37. The IFG is involved in response inhibition, language processing and cognitive control38,39. Dysfunction in these regions has been implicated in various psychiatric disorders40,41. Our findings suggest that these regions form a hub network that is disrupted across disorders.

    Our genetic characterization revealed that the NP factor is genetically associated with ADHD and depression PRSs and linked to neurodevelopmental SNPs. This aligns with prior literature showing that psychiatric disorders share high degrees of genetic correlation and that common genetic variants are involved in neurodevelopmental processes17,18. Our findings extend this literature by linking shared genetic factors to specific neural circuits and cognitive functions.

    The developmental trajectory of the NP factor showed that connectivity patterns associated with psychopathology normalize during development, with the most pronounced changes occurring during adolescence. This suggests that adolescence is a critical period for the maturation of these networks and that delayed development of prefrontal circuits may contribute to psychopathology. This interpretation is supported by our genetic findings, which showed enrichment for neurodevelopmental processes.

    The NP factor predicted persistence of symptoms from adolescence to young adulthood, indicating that it could be used to identify individuals at risk for chronic psychopathology. Early identification of these individuals could enable targeted interventions during the critical period of adolescence, potentially preventing the development of chronic mental disorders.

    Our study has several strengths. First, we used a large, longitudinal neuroimaging cohort with multimodal data (task-based fMRI, resting-state fMRI, genetics, cognitive assessments). Second, we used machine-learning approaches with multiple training–validation pairs to increase reliability and reproducibility. Third, we validated our findings across multiple developmental periods, imaging modalities and clinical samples. Fourth, we characterized the neurobehavioral and genetic underpinnings of the NP factor, providing mechanistic insights.

    Our study also has limitations. First, the IMAGEN cohort is population-based and primarily of European ancestry, limiting generalizability to other populations. Second, the clinical samples (ADHD-200, STRATIFY/ESTRA) had smaller sample sizes and different age ranges, limiting direct comparisons. Third, we focused on a limited set of cognitive functions (executive function, processing speed, memory), and other cognitive domains may also be relevant. Fourth, we did not examine environmental factors, which also contribute to psychopathology. Future studies should address these limitations by including diverse populations, larger clinical samples, broader cognitive assessments and environmental factors.

    In conclusion, we identified a reproducible and general neural basis underlying symptoms of multiple mental health disorders. This NP factor represents a unified, genetically determined, delayed development of prefrontal circuits that leads to poor executive function and increased risk for multiple mental disorders. These findings may help develop new therapeutic interventions for psychiatric comorbidities by targeting shared neural circuits and cognitive functions.

    Methods

    Study protocol

    We investigated the multivariate associations between behavioral symptoms and task-based FC (MID task, SST and emotion reactivity task) with the widely used CPM23,51. The task-based connectome prediction analysis was conducted in the population-based IMAGEN sample (aged 14 years, N = 1,750). The task-based connectomes were constructed from three fMRI tasks: SST (response inhibition), MID (reward processing) and EFT (emotion reactivity). For each task, we extracted time series from 264 predefined ROIs based on the Power atlas52. FC was computed as Pearson correlation between the time series of each pair of ROIs, resulting in a 264 × 264 connectivity matrix for each task.

    Symptoms were measured using standardized scales: ASD (Social Responsiveness Scale), ADHD (Conners' Parent Rating Scale), ODD (Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire), CD (SDQ), GAD (Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders), depression (Mood and Feelings Questionnaire), ED (Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire), SP (SDQ). Symptom scores were z-scored within the sample.

    We constructed eight CPMs, one for each symptom. For each CPM, we used 11 independent training–validation pairs (50% training, 50% validation, stratified by sex) to increase reliability. In each fold, we computed correlations between FC and symptom scores in the training set, selected edges with significant correlations (P < 0.01), and summed the FC strength of these edges to compute a predicted symptom score in the validation set. We computed prediction accuracy as the correlation between predicted and actual symptom scores across folds.

    To identify crossdisorder edges, we extracted edges that were significantly correlated with each symptom across all 11 folds (present in at least 6 folds). We then computed the proportion of edges shared between each pair of symptoms. Shared edges across all eight symptoms were extracted to form the NP factor.

    We computed the NP factor score for each participant by summing the FC strength of all shared edges (positive and negative separately). We computed test–retest reliability of the NP factor across folds using ICC. We computed longitudinal stability by correlating NP factor scores at age 14 with scores at age 19.

    We examined neurobehavioral associations by correlating NP factor scores with cognitive test scores (IQ, working memory, risk adjustment, processing speed, memory consolidation). We examined genetic associations by correlating NP factor scores with PRSs for ADHD and depression. We conducted gene ontology analysis of SNPs associated with NP factor scores using MAGMA53.

    We examined generalizability by computing NP factor scores in independent cohorts: ABCD (ages 10–11), IMAGEN (age 19), HCP (age 29), ADHD-200, STRATIFY/ESTRA. For resting-state generalizability, we computed NP factor scores using resting-state connectomes in the IMAGEN cohort. We examined predictive power by correlating NP factor scores with symptom scores in each cohort.
