# The Impact of PhD Studies on Mental Health — A Longitudinal Population Study

**Authors:** Sanna Bergvall a, Clara Fernström b, Eva Ranehill c, Anna Sandberg d

**Affiliations:**
- a: Department of Economics, University of Gothenburg
- b: Stockholm Business School, Stockholm University
- c: Department of Economics, Lund University
- d: Swedish Institute for Social Research, Stockholm University (Corresponding author)

---

## Abstract

Recent self-reported and cross-sectional survey evidence documents high levels of mental health problems among PhD students. We study the relationship between PhD studies and mental health care utilization using Swedish administrative records of prescriptions for psychiatric medication for the full population of PhD students. We find that prior to starting their PhD studies, prospective students use psychiatric medication at a rate similar to a matched sample of individuals with a master's degree. However, following the start of their PhD studies, psychiatric medication use among PhD students increases substantially. This upward trend continues throughout the course of PhD studies, and by the fifth year medication use has increased by 40 percent compared to pre-PhD levels. After the fifth year, when most students in our sample graduate, the psychiatric medication use declines notably. Heterogeneity analyses indicate that the increase in medication use occurs broadly across different student subgroups and academic disciplines, with the exception of students in the medical and health sciences.

## 1. Introduction

### Background

Recent survey evidence suggests mental health problems are substantially more prevalent among PhD students than in the general population or among other highly educated groups (Levecque et al., 2017; Satinsky et al., 2021; Bolotnyy, Basilico & Barreira, 2022). This evidence highlights an important issue and has spurred a debate about whether there is a mental health crisis in graduate education.

### Research Questions

1. **Representativeness:** To what extent are survey-based estimates representative of the full population of PhD students?
2. **Causality vs. Selection:** To what extent are observed mental health problems among PhD students attributable to PhD education itself, as opposed to selection of individuals with pre-existing mental health challenges?

### Contributions

1. **Population-wide data:** Covers all individuals entering Swedish PhD programs (2006-2017) across all academic fields
2. **Objective measures:** Uses administrative records of medical prescriptions and hospitalizations, not self-reports
3. **Control groups:** Compares trends with relevant control groups matched on sociodemographic characteristics
4. **Longitudinal design:** Tracks health care uptake before, during, and after PhD studies
5. **Mechanism exploration:** Investigates correlations with individual factors (gender, family composition) and institutional factors (research field, faculty-to-student ratio, post-graduation outcomes)

## 2. Institutional Background

### Swedish PhD System

- **Duration:** 4 years of full-time study and research (extendable to 5 years with additional duties)
- **Funding:** Guaranteed funding for full duration since 1998
- **Admissions:** 3,000-4,000 new PhD students annually
- **Gender:** Nearly half (46%) are women
- **Employment:** Majority employed as doctoral candidates by universities with salary and benefits

### Funding Types

1. **University employment (majority):**
   - Salary from university
   - Social insurance protections
   - Paid sick leave, parental leave, pension contributions
   - Fixed-term 4-year contract

2. **Tax-exempt scholarships (smaller, declining share):**
   - No social insurance protections
   - Limited or no paid sick leave or parental leave
   - No pension rights
   - Policy changes (2012-2017) phased out this funding type

3. **External employment:**
   - Hospitals (medical/health sciences)
   - Private companies (engineering/technology)
   - Agreement with university ensures time/resources for PhD studies

### Self-Reported Stress

2016 survey of 4,751 PhD students (Swedish Higher Education Authority):
- 54% reported stress leading to negative experiences (high/very high)
- 59% of women, 50% of men
- Prevalence increases during PhD: 16% (first year) vs 25% (final year)
- Despite stress: 86% rated education "Good" or "Very good"
- 82% would choose PhD again

## 3. Data

### 3.1 Mental Health Care Uptake

#### Primary Outcome: Psychiatric Medication

- **Definition:** Binary indicator (collected psychiatric medication in given year)
- **Includes:** All health care providers in Sweden (primary care, occupational health)
- **Classification:** ATC system (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical)
  - Antidepressants (e.g., SSRIs) - N06A
  - Anxiolytics (e.g., benzodiazepines) - N05B
  - Hypnotics and sedatives (e.g., melatonin) - N05C
- **Source:** Prescribed Drugs Register (available from 2005)

#### Secondary Outcome: Hospitalizations

- **Definition:** Binary indicator (admitted to hospital for mental/behavioral disorder in given year)
- **Captures:** More acute/severe conditions
- **Includes:**
  - Major depression
  - Anxiety disorders
  - Sleep and eating disorders
  - Substance abuse
  - Injuries from self-harm
- **Excludes:** Intellectual disabilities, developmental disorders (unlikely influenced by PhD)
- **Source:** National Patient Register (ICD classification)

### Therapy Limitations

The study does not observe therapy administered in primary care. Focus on medication rather than therapy for two reasons:
1. Therapy accounts for small share of psychiatric treatment in primary care (Stockholm 2011: 8% antidepressants, 9% sleep medication, 6% tranquilizers vs 3% therapy)
2. No systematic differences in care type: 26.2% of medicated PhD students also had specialized outpatient mental health care vs 25.4% in control group

### 3.2 Individual Characteristics

**Demographics:**
- Gender, foreign-born status
- Age at PhD start
- Marital status at PhD start
- Presence of young children at PhD start
- Relocation for PhD studies

**Parental Background:**
- At least one parent holds PhD
- Parental history of psychiatric medication use

**Military Enlistment Records** (~half of men, 1% of women):
- Cognitive ability: IQ (1-9 scale)
- Non-cognitive: Stress tolerance (1-9), leadership ability (1-9)
- Available for 31% PhD sample (IQ), 28% (stress tolerance), 26% (leadership)
- Extended using sibling scores: 48% (IQ), 44% (stress tolerance), 41% (leadership)

**Funding Source:**
- University/company employment vs external grants (scholarships)

### 3.3 Institutional Characteristics

**For each research field × university combination:**

**Program Characteristics:**
- Average time to graduation
- Share of female PhD students
- Share of female professors
- Ratio of professors to PhD students

**Collaboration:**
- Average number of coauthors per paper (Web of Science)

**Post-Graduation Outcomes:**
- Share with registered unemployment after graduation
- Share obtaining academic jobs in Sweden
- Average wage after graduation
- Average wage premium for PhD vs master's degree

## 4. Sample Construction

### Initial Sample
- All individuals starting PhD in Sweden (2006-2017): 37,134
- Requirement: Birth year and gender information available

### Exclusions

1. **No Swedish master's degree:** 12,138 excluded
   - Ensures pre-treatment observation for all
   - Allows construction of matched control group

2. **Not observable year prior to PhD enrollment:** 563 excluded

3. **Low activity level fields:** 4,298 excluded
   - Median activity level <70%
   - Activity = % of full-time employment devoted to PhD studies
   - Removes clinically active medical PhD students pursuing PhD alongside professional practice
   - 98.5% of excluded students are medical/health sciences
   - 56% hold master's in medicine, 57% employed at hospital during PhD

### Final Sample
- **20,085 PhD students**
- Followed over time regardless of completion
- Dropout may be influenced by mental health (endogenous to outcome)

### Control Groups

**Highly Educated Control Group:**
- 306,430 individuals with Swedish master's degree, no PhD studies
- Matched on gender, birth year, field and year of master's degree

**General Population Control Group:**
- 7,045,134 individuals alive in 2006, never enrolled in PhD
- Matched on gender and birth year

## 6. Main Results

### 6.1 Event Study Results

**Primary finding:** Sharp and sustained increase in psychiatric medication following PhD start

**Timeline:**
- **Year -1 (baseline):** Reference period
- **Year 0-5:** Progressive increase
- **Year 5:** 39.5% relative increase (2.5 percentage point increase)
- **After Year 5:** Substantial decline

**Persistence:** Although medication use declines after year 5, it remains elevated 7-10 years after PhD start

### Comparison to Parental Death

Benchmarked against sudden loss of a parent:
- PhD impact: Larger in relative terms, more persistent over time
- Parental death: Acute trauma with different dynamics
- PhD studies: Chronic stressor with sustained impact

### 6.2 Alternative Explanations Tested

#### Correlated Shocks

**Concern:** PhD start coincides with other life events (graduation, job start, relocation)

**Test:** Event study on highly educated control group around university graduation

**Result:** No increase in psychiatric medication around graduation
**Conclusion:** Estimates unlikely driven by correlated life events

#### Healthcare-Seeking Behavior

**Concern:** Estimates reflect increased willingness to seek care, not worse mental health

**Tests:**

1. **Hospitalizations (severe conditions):**
   - Less influenced by care-seeking propensity
   - Base rate: 0.24% hospitalized pre-PhD
   - Finding: Similar pattern to medication, larger relative effect size
   - Conclusion: Reflects genuine deterioration in mental health

2. **Other medication classes:**
   - Analyzed 6 largest ATC pharmacological groups
   - Finding: Modest increases in several classes, but smaller than psychiatric medications
   - Conclusion: Not driven by general increase in healthcare utilization

3. **Other hospitalizations:**
   - Analyzed 6 largest diagnostic categories (excluding mental health)
   - Finding: General increase observed, but mental health consistently larger
   - Conclusion: PhD affects mental health more than other conditions

### 6.3 Robustness Checks

All robust across multiple specifications:

1. **Alternative event study methods:**
   - Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024)
   - Sun & Abraham (2021)
   - Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021)

2. **Sample restrictions:**
   - Removed Swedish master's degree requirement
   - Alternative activity level thresholds

3. **Alternative control group:**
   - Stacked Diff-in-Diff comparing PhD students to future PhD students (start 1-5 years later)

4. **Sample composition over time:**
   - Balanced panels (7 years after PhD start)
   - 5 years before + 7 years after

5. **Master's to PhD timing:**
   - Split sample: ≤2 years vs >2 years between master's and PhD
   - Both groups show increase, but larger for those starting soon after

### 7.1 Individual Factors

**Overall pattern:** Effect widespread across nearly all subgroups, but magnitude varies

| Factor | Subgroup | Relative Increase | p-value |
|--------|----------|-------------------|---------|
| **Gender** | Men | 28% | - |
| | Women | 16% | 0.011 |
| **Birthplace** | Foreign-born | 36% | - |
| | Sweden-born | 17% | 0.011 |
| **Age** | <29 years | 34% | - |
| | ≥29 years | 14% | <0.001 |
| **Relocation** | Relocated | 39% | - |
| | Stayed | 20% | 0.045 |
| **Funding** | PhD employment | 27% | - |
| | Scholarships | 16% | 0.095 |
| | Company employment | 0% | 0.007 |
| | Other | 8% | 0.006 |
| **Parent education** | Parent has PhD | Similar | NS |
| | No parent PhD | Similar | NS |
| **Parent mental health** | Parent used meds | Similar | NS |
| | No parent meds | Similar | NS |
| **Family status** | Married | Similar | NS |
| | Unmarried | Similar | NS |
| | Has children | Similar | NS |
| | No children | Similar | NS |

**Cognitive/Non-Cognitive (military enlistment):**

| Trait | High vs Low | Difference |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| **Leadership ability** | Highest (13%) vs Lowest (39%) | p=0.031 |
| **Stress tolerance** | Slightly smaller effect with higher tolerance | NS |
| **IQ** | Slightly smaller effect with higher IQ | NS |

**Important nuance:** Some differences reflect baseline rates. Absolute increases in percentage points are often similar across groups.

### 7.2 Institutional Factors

#### Research Fields

**Significant increases in all fields EXCEPT medical/health sciences**

| Field | Relative Increase |
|-------|-------------------|
| Natural sciences | Largest |
| Engineering & technology | Largest |
| Social sciences | Significant |
| Humanities | Significant |
| **Medical & health sciences** | **No significant increase** |

**Finer breakdown (OECD FORD 3-digit):**
- 84% of estimated coefficients positive
- 45% statistically significant at 5% level
- No field shows statistically significant negative effect

#### Institutional Environment

**Patterns observed:**

| Characteristic | Largest Increase | Protective Factors |
|----------------|------------------|--------------------|
| Professor-to-PhD student ratio | Low (≤0.17) | Higher ratio |
| Female professors | Low share (≤10%) | Higher female share |
| Female PhD students | Low share (≤38%) | Higher female share |
| Time to graduation | Longer average | Shorter average |

**Surprising finding:** Post-graduation outcomes not strongly associated:
- Unemployment rates
- Probability of staying in academia
- Average wages
- Wage premium for PhD

### 7.3 Medical & Health Sciences Exception

**Speculated explanations:**

1. **Higher pre-PhD stress:** Medical studies may be more stressful before PhD enrollment
2. **Different support structures:** Better mentorship or support systems
3. **Healthcare access:** Colleagues' medical expertise or easier access to care
4. **Earlier diagnosis:** Lower stigma or higher mental health literacy → earlier treatment before PhD
5. **Selection:** Different student characteristics (higher stress tolerance, leadership)
6. **Employment:** Many employed by hospitals alongside PhD studies

**Observed differences:**
- Higher stress tolerance and leadership skills
- Larger share of parents with PhD
- Less frequent relocation
- Shorter time to degree

## 8. Discussion

### Key Findings

1. **No selection effect:** Pre-PhD psychiatric medication use similar to master's degree holders
2. **Causal impact:** Sharp increase at PhD onset, progressive deterioration
3. **Recovery:** Decline after year 5 (graduation), suggesting situational/transient effect
4. **Widespread:** Affects nearly all groups, except medical/health sciences
5. **Institutional factors:** Worse outcomes with low faculty ratios, low female representation, long time-to-degree

### Methodological Strengths

1. **Population-wide:** Full population, no selection bias
2. **Objective measures:** Administrative records, not self-reports
3. **Longitudinal:** Controls for time-invariant individual characteristics
4. **Panel structure:** Rules out pre-existing mental health conditions
5. **Causal inference:** Event study with individual and calendar-year fixed effects

### Limitations

1. **Underestimation:** Only captures individuals who seek care; many with symptoms don't seek help
2. **Help-seeking bias:** Treatment rates vary by demographics (women, highly educated, Caucasians, unmarried higher)
   - May explain some heterogeneity (e.g., men, foreign-born)
   - May explain medical sciences exception (lower stigma, higher mental health literacy)
3. **Stigma:** Key barrier to care (Clement et al., 2015)
4. **Generalizability:** Swedish context with universal healthcare
   - However, levels broadly consistent with international surveys

### International Comparisons

**Swedish PhD students on psychiatric medication:**
- 13.5% in 2016

**International surveys:**
- US economics PhD students: 14.9% treatment (Bolotnyy et al., 2022)
- Political science PhD students: 10-13.5% treatment (Almasri et al., 2022)
- Meta-analysis of PhD students: 24% report depression/anxiety symptoms (Satinsky et al., 2021)

**Alignment:** Screening tools overestimate depression prevalence by ~2x compared to clinical interviews. Our estimates appear consistent with survey evidence.

### Policy Implications

**Institutional reforms suggested:**

1. **Adequate supervisor resources:** Increase faculty-to-student ratios
2. **Clear milestones:** Provide program structures to reduce prolonged uncertainty
3. **Gender diversity:** Promote gender balance for equity AND well-being
   - Extremely low female representation may foster norms discouraging help-seeking
4. **Relocation support:** Stronger onboarding, peer mentoring, housing support
5. **Early intervention:** Mental health deteriorates from PhD start, not just later stages

**Broader concerns:**
- PhD mental health impacts academic productivity
- Contributes to selection favoring resilience over aptitude
- Raises concerns about researcher pipeline

---

## 9. Conclusion

**Main conclusion:** PhD studies are associated with significant and substantial increase in mental health care utilization

**Evidence:**
- **39.5% increase** in psychiatric medication use by year 5
- Increase observed broadly across groups (except medical/health sciences)
- Not driven by correlated shocks or healthcare-seeking behavior
- Affects full distribution of mental health outcomes (medication + hospitalizations)

**Interpretation:** Consistent with negative impact of PhD studies on mental health

**Message to institutions:** Need for comprehensive policies to improve work environment for early-career researchers
