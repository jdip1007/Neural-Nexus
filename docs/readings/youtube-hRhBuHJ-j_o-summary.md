## Key Points
- This is a story about taking a 1970 Lincoln Continental Mark III, a car with only about 12,000 mi on it from new, a car that still looks like it slipped through a wormhole from Nixon's first term, and adding an otherwise invisible modern safety system without drilling any holes in it, without hanging some ugly plastic wart in the rear window without making it look like somebody attacked the classic luxury car with the RGB lighting aisle from a micro center
- And then on the other side, you've got a modern ESP32 microcontroller running at hundreds of millions of cycles per second, driving individually addressable LEDs, parsing timing windows measured in milliseconds, and making decisions about whether the car is breaking, turning left, turning right, backing up, or doing some combination of those things all at the same time
- If you're doing this on a modern car, you might be tempted to go looking for a CAN bus message because there might be a nice little packet somewhere that says the brake pedal is down, the left signal is active, the right signal is active, reverse is selected, and maybe even what radio station the driver is listening to
- It's also a story about translation because on one side you've got a 55-year-old electrical system built from switches, bulbs, flashers, copper wire, and the kind of cultural optimism that says a seatback ending halfway up your spine is probably all the neck protection a tough guy like me really needs
- It's a nominal 12vt system, which means it might be 12 volts or maybe even 11 with the engine off, closer to 14 or 15 when the alternator is charging, lower during cranking, and full of spikes, dips, inductive nonsense, and the general electric mood swings of an old vehicle
- But unless you have the right equipment, the phototric testing, the mounting validation, and all the rest of the whole certification process, you should not pretend that your garage project is suddenly a certified automotive lighting component
