# Autism Spectrum Disorder and IQ – A Complex Interplay

**Authors:** Wolff N, Stroth S, Kamp-Becker I, Roepke S, Roessner V (2022)
**Journal:** Frontiers in Psychiatry, 13, Article 856084
**DOI:** 10.3389/fpsyt.2022.856084

## Executive Summary

- ASD heterogeneity is partly determined by IQ differences; the IQ distribution in ASD has shifted over time from majority cognitive impairment to a more balanced distribution
- Epidemiological vs clinical studies show divergent IQ profiles — clinical samples report more above-average IQ individuals due to referral and sampling biases
- IQ measurement validity in ASD is compromised by test format demands that overlap with ASD core deficits (social communication, flexibility), creating circularity in diagnostic assessment
- Bimodal IQ distribution observed in specialized outpatient clinics: ~38% below average (IQ < 85), ~40% above average (IQ > 115), ~22% average

## Key Findings

1. **Historical shift in IQ distribution:** Early epidemiological studies (1966–2001) reported 40–100% of ASD individuals with cognitive impairment (IQ < 70, mean 70%). More recent studies show decline to ~31% with IQ < 70, with 44% in average-to-above range.

2. **Epidemiological vs clinical discrepancy:** Clinical studies consistently report higher proportions of above-average IQ in ASD compared to epidemiological studies, reflecting referral bias (specialized clinics attract higher-IQ, later-diagnosed individuals).

3. **Bimodal clinical distribution:** In specialized ASD outpatient clinics, researchers observed a bimodal IQ distribution — clusters at both low and high IQ extremes, with fewer individuals in the average range.

4. **IQ test validity concerns:** Standard IQ tests (e.g., Wechsler scales) require abilities impaired in ASD (verbal comprehension, processing speed, social understanding). This makes IQ scores potentially underestimating true cognitive capacity in ASD.

5. **ADOS-IQ circularity:** The Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) is calibrated differently across IQ levels, and IQ influences ADOS scores. This creates reciprocal influence between diagnostic instrument and IQ measure.

6. **Diagnostic delay by IQ level:** Higher-IQ individuals receive ASD diagnosis later (often adulthood), while lower-IQ individuals are diagnosed in childhood. This has implications for support access and identity development.

7. **Sample biases identified:**
   - Referral bias: Specialized clinics see more high-IQ ASD individuals
   - Diagnostic bias: IQ influences who gets diagnosed and when
   - Reporting bias: Epidemiological studies often collapse above-average IQ data

## Clinical Implications

- IQ should not be treated as a fixed trait in ASD but as an interacting factor that influences diagnosis, support needs, and outcomes
- Diagnostic instruments need IQ-stratified norms and awareness of IQ-test overlap with ASD core deficits
- Later-diagnosed (often higher-IQ) individuals face unique challenges: missed childhood support, identity disruption, comorbid mental health conditions
- Service provision must account for the full IQ spectrum, not just the cognitive impairment end
