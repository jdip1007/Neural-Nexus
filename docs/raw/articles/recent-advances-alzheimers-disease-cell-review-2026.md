# **Recent advances in Alzheimer's disease: From molecular mechanisms to therapeutic strategies** 

#### Michelle D. Rudman,<sup>1</sup> Jason D. Ulrich,<sup>1</sup> and David M. Holtzman<sup>1,</sup> * 

1Washington University, Department of Neurology, Hope Center for Neurological Disorders, Knight Alzheimer's Disease Research Center, St. Louis, MO 63110, USA 

*Correspondence: holtzman@wustl.edu https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.06.006 

##### SUMMARY 

Alzheimer's disease (AD) remains the leading cause of dementia worldwide and an escalating global health crisis. The hallmark amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) are now known to be accompanied by a complex array of pathologies that culminate in neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. New disease-modifying therapies for AD can now slow cognitive decline through the removal of amyloid plaques from the brain, but treatments to stop or prevent cognitive impairment remain elusive. In this review, we summarize the most recent updates in AD research on pathologic disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies, highlighting advancements in apolipoprotein E (APOE) biology, neuroimmunology, biomarker discovery, and initial experience with new disease-modifying therapies. These important discoveries are revolutionizing AD diagnosis and treatment and provide hope for a future where AD is not only treatable but also preventable. 

##### INTRODUCTION 

Incredible progress has been made in furthering our understanding of the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of Alzheimer's disease (AD) in the last 5 years. Key advances include the incorporation of sensitive and specific biomarkers in both clinical research and diagnosis; the introduction of the first diseasemodifying therapies; the discovery of rare protective variants in apolipoprotein E (APOE), which have led to breakthroughs in understanding the role of APOE in modulating AD risk; and the recognition of not only the importance of the innate but also the adaptive immune system in contributing to neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in the setting of AD-related pathology. In this review, we highlight these and other recent advances that are reshaping our understanding of AD pathogenesis and transforming clinical diagnostic and therapeutic paradigms. 

##### PATHOLOGICAL MECHANISMS 

##### Amyloid 

Amyloid-β (Aβ) is the primary constituent of neuritic plaques. It is produced by cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) sequentially by β-secretase (BACE1) and gamma-secretase (γ-secretase) and released from neurons in a manner modulated by synaptic activity. Aβ production is also regulated by sortilinrelated receptor-1 (SORL1), a neuronal receptor that regulates APP trafficking to decrease its conversion into Aβ, and loss-offunction mutations in _SORL1_ are an important genetic risk factor for AD.<sup>1</sup> Accumulation of Aβ begins in the brain ∼20 years before the onset of cognitive impairment and is a critical first step in a cascade that ultimately leads to AD. Over the last 5 years, research on Aβ in AD has undergone a significant transformation. New clinical trial successes have validated the idea that removal of pathologic Aβ from the brain can slow the progression of AD, and a more nuanced understanding of the complexity of Aβ species has opened the door to the development of new diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

In AD and many neurodegenerative diseases, proteins that form amyloids are central drivers of neurodegeneration. Recent advances in cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) have enabled high-resolution determination of disease-specific conformations of amyloidogenic proteins in AD and related dementias. Cryo-EM studies have now resolved human brain-derived Aβ42 filaments and identified structural differences between sporadic and familial AD, as well as both shared and novel filaments in AD that occurs in Down syndrome (DSAD).<sup>2,3</sup> Further, sAD brain-derived Aβ42 structures are distinct from those of Aβ40 filaments.<sup>4,5</sup> These studies highlight previously unappreciated heterogeneity in Aβ species with important implications for disease modeling, pathogenic mechanisms, and therapeutic targeting. Importantly, both sporadic and familial AD brainderived Aβ42 filaments differ markedly from synthetically derived fibrils in side-chain orientations, side-chain contacts, and interprotofilament packing,<sup>2</sup> calling into question the utility of these synthetically derived fibrils in disease modeling. By contrast, Aβ42 filaments from the _App_<sup>NL-F</sup> knockin mouse model of amyloidosis were confirmed to be identical to human brainderived filaments from DIAD cases,<sup>2</sup> though caution is still warranted in extrapolating to sAD disease mechanisms. These newly elucidated disease-specific Aβ conformations may now facilitate research into the pathogenic implications of Aβ structural heterogeneity, including activation of distinct biological pathways and cellular and regional susceptibility to disease across neurodegenerative diseases, as well as more targeted development of AD biomarkers and therapeutics.

Historically, multiple lines of evidence suggested that Aβ aggregation and accumulation in the brain are the necessary first steps and driving factors for the development of AD. Rare genetic mutations in _APP_ , _PSEN1_ , and _PSEN2_ affecting APP levels, processing, and sequence are causative for dominantlyinherited AD (DIAD). DIAD is a rare form of AD typically characterized by a very early age of symptomatic onset (ranging from about the 4<sup>th</sup> to 6<sup>th</sup> decade of life on average<sup>6</sup> ) that varies depending on the specific gene mutation. However, neuropathologically DIAD and late-onset sporadic AD (sAD) share a similar progression of AD biomarkers indicative of a shared pathological process.<sup>7</sup> Importantly, in both DIAD and sAD, amyloid pathology precedes other major pathological changes in AD, and key risk factors for sAD, including _APOE_ genotype, age, and sleep, appear to modify AD risk largely by modulating Aβ aggregation and clearance.<sup>8</sup> However, Aβ pathology alone is insufficient to cause AD, and cognitive decline correlates instead with tau pathology. Decades of clinical trials targeting Aβ pathology in sAD had previously failed to show benefit. In 2023, the first clear evidence of clinical benefit targeting Aβ pathology with anti-Aβ antibodies was reported in the CLARITY-AD phase 3 trial of lecanemab for treatment of early clinical stage AD.<sup>9</sup> This was followed closely by a similar report of clinical benefit in the phase 3 clinical trial TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 of donanemab in early AD.<sup>10</sup> In both cases, rapid and robust removal of fibrillar Aβ by anti-Aβ antibodies resulted in a mean slowing of cognitive decline of ∼30% over 18 months. In addition to representing a major step forward in the treatment of AD, these results demonstrate that reducing amyloid pathology in sAD can slow the disease course. Not only did Aβ removal slow cognitive decline, but it also slowed progression of tau pathology (as measured either by tau positron-emission tomography [PET] or fluid biomarkers of phosphorylated tau [p-tau] species) and attenuated neuroinflammation (as measured by biomarkers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP]) with correlations with the magnitude of amyloid removal demonstrating dose-dependent effects.<sup>9,11,12</sup> These data further support a causative role for Aβ in driving additional pathologies, including tau pathology and neuroinflammation in AD.

Mechanistically, studies indicate that Aβ clearance by anti-Aβ antibodies is dependent upon Fc-mediated microglial activation and phagocytosis.<sup>13,14</sup> This might explain why crenezumab, an IgG4 antibody with intentionally reduced effector function to mitigate the risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), failed to reduce Aβ plaques and produce clinical benefit.<sup>15</sup> Anti-amyloid antibodies have also lent additional insight into the species of Aβ that are most pathogenic. Aβ monomers, particularly the more aggregation-prone Aβ42 species, may spontaneously self-aggregate first into oligomers, then intermediate species known as protofibrils, before developing into mature fibrils, which arrange into cross-β-pleated sheet structures to form insoluble amyloid plaques. As aggregates age, they undergo post-translational modifications, including N-terminal pyroglutamate modification (pE3-Aβ) and phosphorylation (pSer8Aβ). Current evidence from large-scale clinical trials supports a pathogenic role for Aβ protofibrils and mature plaques containing pE3-Aβ based on the successes of lecanemab and donanemab, respectively.<sup>9,10</sup> Conversely, antibodies targeting Aβ monomers failed to show benefit in clinical trials (solanezumab and, in part, crenezumab),<sup>15,16</sup> and outcomes for aducanumab, which preferentially binds fibrils, were equivocal. However, it is worth noting that lecanemab and donanemab also cleared Aβ plaques more rapidly and robustly compared with previous anti-Aβ antibodies, making it difficult to know if the therapeutic effect is attributable to a particular Aβ species versus the speed and/or magnitude of Aβ clearance.

The results of CLARITY-AD and TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 also highlight an important disconnect between the robust removal of Aβ and comparatively modest slowing of disease progression based on both clinical decline and biomarkers. This is believed to be attributable to the presence of multiple significant co-pathologies, including pathologic tau species, neuroinflammation, and lipid dyshomeostasis, among others, whose initial development is dependent on seeding and spreading of Aβ but later acquire a semi-autonomous and self-perpetuating nature, resulting in ongoing neurodegeneration even after the removal of Aβ. The accumulation of these co-pathologies with disease progression is one reason that earlier intervention with anti-amyloid therapeutics is thought to confer more benefit, as was observed in the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial, which stratified participants by tau-PET status,<sup>10</sup> and is also the premise for the development of complementary therapeutics targeting other aspects of AD pathology.

##### Tau 

In AD, tau becomes hyperphosphorylated, leading to impaired microtubule binding, misfolding, and aggregation. The resulting formation of filaments, NFT, and other aggregated species contributes—through direct and indirect mechanisms—to neuronal dysfunction and cell death. Tau exists as six isoforms generated by alternative splicing of the _MAPT_ gene, classified by the number of N-terminal inserts (0, 1, or 2) and the presence of three or four microtubule-binding repeats (3R or 4R). Both 3R and 4R isoforms are present in AD tau aggregates. The progressive accumulation and propagation of tau pathology, particularly its spread from the entorhinal cortex to the hippocampus and neocortex, correlates strongly with cognitive impairment.<sup>17</sup> Recent major advances have clarified the structural heterogeneity of pathologic tau across tauopathies, the role of post-translational modifications, and mechanisms of tau propagation.

Application of cryo-EM to tauopathies has revealed that different diseases are defined by unique pathologic tau structures, offering insight into why these different tauopathies produce clinically distinct syndromes.<sup>18</sup> The cryo-EM structure of tau filaments from sAD was first reported in 2017, with the core composed primarily of the R3 and R4 microtubule-binding repeat (MTBR) domains from paired protofilaments and a fuzzy coat formed from the disordered N and C termini.<sup>19</sup> The structure of insoluble AD tau filaments is identical to that found in primary age-related tauopathy (PART) but distinct from those found in chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), Pick's disease (PiD), corticobasal degeneration (CBD), and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP).<sup>18</sup> Importantly, the structure of AD tau filaments was replicated using recombinant tau _in vitro_ , providing a reliable model system for studying AD-relevant tau dynamics.<sup>20</sup> Using this _in vitro_ system, time-resolved cryo-EM suggests that diverse tau filament structures can arise from a common misfolded precursor, with interacting proteins and biochemical conditions shaping disease-specific conformations.<sup>21</sup> Soluble tau oligomers have also recently been reported to have distinct conformational characteristics and post-translational modifications in different tauopathies, with long, fibrillar aggregates enriched in phospho-epitopes observed in AD as compared with shorter round aggregates in PSP and PiD, as well as selective phosphorylation at serine 356 in PSP.<sup>22</sup> These disease-specific differences in soluble tau oligomers may ultimately confer utility as potential biomarkers or disease-specific therapeutic targets, particularly given evidence that oligomeric species may accumulate early at synapses and play a role in trans-synaptic spread and neurotoxicity.<sup>23</sup> Elucidating structural mechanisms of pathologic tau aggregation and which tau aggregates drive toxicity (e.g., small oligomers versus fibrils) will be critical for understanding clinical heterogeneity in tauopathies and for guiding the development of targeted tau aggregation inhibitors.

Tau in AD exhibits a diverse array of post-translational modifications that likely regulate its aggregation, degradation, microtubule binding, and neurotoxicity. High-resolution proteomic mapping has identified more than 95 distinct modifications across 88 amino acid residues on AD tau, including previously underexplored sites.<sup>24</sup> These results reveal specific combinations and ordered addition of post-translational modifications associated with tau aggregation, disease stage, and patient heterogeneity in AD. Unique signatures of post-translational modifications are also observed in insoluble tau filaments from different tauopathies, with disease-specific modifications particularly concentrated in the MTBR, suggesting a possible role in the formation of the unique filament cores.<sup>25</sup> Tau hyperphosphorylation has long been a central focus of AD research. Longitudinal biomarker studies reveal stereotyped changes in p-tau across the AD disease spectrum, with increases in some p-tau species correlating with the earliest stages of Aβ deposition (p-tau217, p-tau181) and others with later stages of disease, such as brain atrophy and hypometabolism (p-tau205).<sup>26</sup> _In vitro_ study of recombinant tau suggests C-terminal phosphorylation is required for filament assembly into an AD conformation, and phosphorylation of the proline-rich region is also more prevalent in AD tau and may facilitate tau fibrillization by neutralizing the positively charged MTBR.<sup>20,24</sup> Tau acetylation also correlates with pathological tau burden and disease progression, and acetylation sites in the MTBR may promote filament formation similarly to phosphorylation.<sup>24,27</sup> Tau acetylation by the p300 and CREB-binding protein (CBP) acetyltransferases reduces tau degradation, enhances neurotoxicity, and disrupts synaptic and axonal function.<sup>28–30</sup> Acetylation may inhibit tau degradation by directly competing with ubiquitination for tau residues.<sup>31</sup> Recently, p300/CBP-driven acetylation was also shown to facilitate tau secretion and pathological spreading _in vivo_ .<sup>32</sup> Collectively, these findings support p300/CBP inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy for AD, although clinical validation remains limited (NCT03277573).<sup>33,34</sup>

Molecular mechanisms underpinning pathologic tau accumulation and degradation remain elusive. CRISPRi screens of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons expressing tau V337M, a causal mutant for frontotemporal dementia (FTD), found that knockdown of genes in the autophagy and ubiquitinproteasome pathways increased tau oligomer levels. In particular, the cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase cullin-5 (CUL5) strongly regulated tau levels specifically in the neuronal soma.<sup>35</sup> A role for CUL5 in tau proteostasis was further supported by the observation that CUL5 and associated ubiquitin complex protein expression strongly correlated with resilience of vulnerable excitatory and somatostatin-positive neuron populations in AD.<sup>35</sup> Similar CRISPRi approaches were employed to identify genes that modified seeding of tau inclusions in iPSC-derived neurons expressing 4R tau or 4R tau with a P301S mutation. Tau seeding and propagation in this model were associated with dysfunctional endolysosomal systems, including accumulation of multilamellar bodies, and were augmented by increased neuronal activity. Along these lines, CRISPRi screening found that knockdown of genes associated with the retromer complex, particularly vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 29 (VPS29), increased tau inclusions. Interestingly, the UFMylation pathway, involved in ER stress responses, was implicated in both of these CRISPRi screens, where knockdown of UFMylation-related genes reduced tau oligomers and tau propagation.<sup>35,36</sup> UFMylation was also strongly correlated with pathological tau in frontal and temporal AD cortex, although the mechanistic link is unclear.<sup>37</sup> Further, aggregation-prone proteins such as tau and α-synuclein can be mono-UFMylated, which can in turn promote unconventional protein secretion.<sup>38</sup>

The development of novel targeted protein degradation strategies has led to new approaches to promote clearance of pathologic tau species. Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) are bispecific molecules that have been engineered to induce selective degradation of tau by bringing tau in close proximity to an E3 ubiquitin ligase to promote ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation. Likewise, autophagy-targeting chimeras (AUTOTACs) link tau-binding modules to an autophagytargeting domain for degradation by the autophagy-lysosome pathway. Several PROTACs and AUTOTACs have shown efficacy in reducing tau accumulation _in vitro_ and in mouse models of tauopathy<sup>39–41</sup> with some also demonstrating the ability to specifically reduce pathologic tau.<sup>42–44</sup> Another approach for promoting selective degradation of aggregated tau utilizes a fusion protein with the RING domain of an E3 ligase. The tau-targeting-RING fusion protein is able to dimerize and activate its E3 function upon binding tau aggregates, thereby promoting selective degradation of aggregated tau via the ubiquitin-proteasome system. This system has shown efficacy in reducing tau aggregates in mouse models of tauopathy.<sup>45,46</sup> In summary, the disruption of protein degradation pathways in AD may exacerbate pathologic tau accumulation, and targeted protein degradation therapeutics represent a promising approach for the removal of toxic intracellular tau aggregates in AD and related tauopathies.

Tau pathology in AD spreads primarily through trans-synaptic transfer along connected neuronal networks, initiated by seeding and propagation from the entorhinal cortex to the hippocampus and then to the neocortex.<sup>47</sup> Both cell-autonomous and non-cell-autonomous mechanisms contribute to tau pathology progression. Cell-autonomous mechanisms include tau-induced synaptic dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, and direct neurotoxicity.<sup>48</sup> Non-cell-autonomous mechanisms involve glial cells. Microglia and astrocytes contribute to tau spread through various mechanisms, including secretion of inflammatory mediators, phagocytic clearance or failure thereof, and direct uptake and spread of pathological tau species.<sup>49,50</sup> Emerging evidence suggests that tau pathology may also spread via extracellular vesicles (exosomes), which can carry pathological tau between cells.<sup>51</sup> Understanding these propagation mechanisms is critical for developing strategies to halt tau pathology progression.
