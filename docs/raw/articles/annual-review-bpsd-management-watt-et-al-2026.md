Management of Behavioral and Psychological Symptoms of Dementia: Nonpharmacologic and Pharmacologic Interventions

Jennifer A. Watt, Zahinoor Ismail, Natasha Lane, Dallas Seitz, and Zahra Goodarzi
Annual Review of Clinical Psychology, 2026, 22:397-425
First published as a Review in Advance on February 12, 2026
https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-061724-085912

Abstract

Mounting evidence supports the efficacy of nonpharmacologic or psychosocial interventions for reducing behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), identifies the comparative risks and benefits of psychotropic medication, and underscores the importance of deprescribing psychotropic medications. However, evidence from clinical settings indicates that uptake of nonpharmacologic interventions and potential overuse of medications remain problematic. We begin by discussing the importance of exploring potential contributors to BPSD, using person-centered language to describe BPSD, implementing holistic and person-centered care plans, measuring clinically important changes in BPSD, and considering social determinants of health when assessing and managing BPSD; we also discuss the historical context informing how clinicians manage BPSD. Next, we compare and contrast nonpharmacologic and pharmacologic approaches to managing BPSD, evaluate their individual and comparative efficacy, and describe recommendations for intervention deprescribing or deimplementation. Lastly, we discuss strengths and limitations of the current evidence supporting BPSD management as well as recommendations for future research.

OVERVIEW

People with dementia manifest neuropsychiatric symptoms, commonly known as BPSD (behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia). BPSD manifest in all stages and types of dementia; 97% of people with dementia experience at least one BPSD within 5 years of diagnosis. Apathy, agitation, aggression, anxiety, irritability, depression, sleep and nighttime disturbances, aberrant motor behavior, disinhibition, appetite and eating changes, delusions, hallucinations, and euphoria can all be manifestations of BPSD.

We have an incomplete understanding of what causes BPSD, but personality, genetics, environmental factors, unmet needs, and anatomical and biochemical changes have been proposed as potential causes. BPSD are associated with greater cognitive and functional decline and heightened caregiver distress; clinicians and caregivers treat BPSD with nonpharmacologic interventions, medications, or both to alleviate the adverse outcomes associated with BPSD.

There is a growing body of literature describing the efficacy of pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions for BPSD management. However, the comparative efficacy of pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions is less well-described, and evidence informing how to best manage BPSD is evolving. Nonpharmacologic interventions can be broadly classified into four categories: psychological (e.g., reminiscence therapy), behavioral (e.g., modification of activities of daily living), sensory (e.g., aromatherapy), and environmental (e.g., outdoor activities). Some nonpharmacologic interventions can be classified into multiple categories or incorporate intervention components from multiple categories. Medications studied for treating BPSD include antipsychotics, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, cholinesterase inhibitors, N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists (i.e., memantine), anxiolytics/hypnotics, and other drugs (e.g., dextromethorphan-quinidine, cannabinoids), although approaches vary widely.

Well-founded concerns about potential harms (e.g., all-cause mortality with antipsychotics) have led people with dementia, family and friend caregivers, and clinicians to search for safer approaches. Particularly attractive are holistic and person-centered BPSD management plans that aim to minimize medication-related harms by maximizing nonpharmacologic intervention use and deprescribing medications that are ineffective or harmful. Implementation of nonpharmacologic interventions for managing BPSD in routine clinical care is a critical ongoing challenge and focus of research.

EXPLORING MEANING IN BPSD TO INTEGRATE NONPHARMACOLOGIC INTERVENTIONS INTO CARE PLANS

How we care for and study people with dementia experiencing BPSD is situated within the person-centered dementia care model, with a goal to preserve personhood and dignity. Conceptual frameworks and empirical evidence describe how behavioral and psychological symptoms in people with dementia can have meaning. Personal (i.e., intrinsic to the individual), social, and environmental factors contribute to the well-being of people with dementia, thus influencing whether they respond to others in their environment to fulfill an unmet need or manifest psychological symptoms in response to how they feel.

Contributory factors include unmet needs for food, affection, or meaningful activity; experiences of pain; social stressors, such as communication difficulties between a person with dementia and their caregiver; and suboptimal environmental conditions, such as a room that is too hot or cold. Understanding the lifelong behavioral pattern of a person with dementia may also provide essential context for their current behavior.

Syntheses of empirical evidence demonstrate a causal association between participating in interventions that better align personal, social, and environmental factors with the needs of people with dementia and their caregivers and reduced BPSD. Multidisciplinary assessments with management plans reduce symptoms of agitation and depression in people with dementia. However, not all empirical studies have demonstrated a clinically meaningful improvement in BPSD associated with participating in tailored nonpharmacologic interventions. Thus, our current understanding of predisposing, protective, perpetuating, and precipitating factors for BPSD is incomplete, but both theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence suggest that at least some behavioral and psychological symptoms have meaning.

SHIFT TOWARD MORE INCLUSIVE LANGUAGE DESCRIBING BPSD

A variety of terminology is used to describe BPSD, including neuropsychiatric symptoms, behavioral changes, changed behaviors, responsive behaviors, and personal expressions. While the terminology for describing BPSD varies, the most common terms in clinical practice guidelines are BPSD and neuropsychiatric symptoms. BPSD are broken down into specific domains or symptom clusters. However, as there is greater adoption of the person-centered model of dementia care, a shift toward more person-centered language is emerging in the medical literature and at the point of care.

The person-centered language paradigm asserts that people with dementia are responding to a need, feeling, or their environment (i.e., responsive behaviors) and that language should be contextually appropriate and inclusive of people with dementia and their caregivers. Caregivers and people with dementia may prefer terminology that reflects the person-centered language paradigm as opposed to language used in the medical literature. Further, the person-centered language paradigm favors describing the nature of a behavior (e.g., describing how a person with dementia calls out requesting food as opposed to simply saying the person with dementia is agitated), which promotes development of nonpharmacologic interventions to meet the needs of people with dementia.

This divergence in terminology being applied in medical research and at the point of care creates challenges regarding how behavioral and psychological symptoms ought to be described, necessitating mapping of terms between these two paradigms when developing evidence-based care plans. Person-centered language also emphasizes describing behaviors (e.g., pacing, restless) rather than labeling an individual (e.g., they are aggressive). The challenge, however, lies with measurement; valid and reliable outcome measures, ideally based on a syndromic understanding of the behaviors operationalized with diagnostic criteria, are required to properly assess efficacy of interventions. Addressing this challenge is essential to enabling the use of nonpharmacologic approaches and reconciling disparate terminologies for behavioral changes in people with dementia.

NEED FOR PROACTIVE, TIERED, AND HOLISTIC BPSD ASSESSMENT AND MANAGEMENT APPROACHES

Holistic approaches for assessing people with BPSD are needed to understand the potential role of nonpharmacologic interventions, medications, or both in developing a care plan that recognizes and respects the personhood of an individual with dementia. Holistic approaches addressing potential protective, predisposing, perpetuating, and precipitating factors for BPSD require input from people with dementia, family or friend caregivers, and clinicians.

Care plans should not only be developed in reaction to observed behaviors and symptoms but also be proactive to prevent or lessen behavior and symptom burden. Further, tiered care plans support clinicians and family and friend caregivers to appropriately respond to changes in symptoms and behaviors (e.g., implement nonpharmacologic interventions before medications unless there is a risk of harm to self or others).

As an example, the DICE (describe, investigate, create, and evaluate) approach prompts clinicians to (a) contextualize and characterize the concerning behavior or psychological symptom; (b) investigate potential patient, caregiver, and environmental contributing factors; (c) collaborate with formal and informal caregivers to create a care plan that both addresses contributing factors and includes tiered, nonpharmacologic interventions before considering pharmacologic options; and (d) evaluate the effectiveness of the care plan.

A proactive, tiered, holistic, and co-created BPSD assessment and management approach aligns with person-centered dementia care models and has been associated with reductions in BPSD. Creating care plans co-created by multiple health disciplines, family or friend caregivers, and people with dementia (when possible) is crucial because understanding the meaning in behavior requires contextual information that can be provided only by those who know the person with dementia best.

MEASURING CLINICALLY IMPORTANT CHANGES IN BPSD

Measuring clinically important changes in BPSD is essential to determine whether interventions are effective. Outcome measures for BPSD include global measures that assess all neuropsychiatric symptoms, measures that target specific symptoms (e.g., agitation, depression), and measures that assess quality of life. Global measures of BPSD include the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), which is widely used in research and clinical practice.

The NPI assesses 12 neuropsychiatric symptom domains: delusions, hallucinations, agitation/aggression, depression/dysphoria, anxiety, elation/euphoria, apathy/indifference, disinhibition, irritability/lability, aberrant motor behavior, nighttime behavior disturbances, and appetite and eating changes. Each domain is assessed for frequency, severity, and caregiver distress. The NPI has been validated in multiple languages and is sensitive to change.

Measures targeting specific symptoms include scales such as the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory (CMAI) for agitation, the Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia (CSDD) for depression, and the Apathy Evaluation Scale (AES) for apathy. These scales provide more detailed assessment of specific symptoms and may be more sensitive to change in those domains.

Goal attainment scaling is another approach to measuring clinically important change. This approach involves setting personalized goals with people with dementia and their caregivers and measuring progress toward those goals. This approach is particularly useful for assessing person-centered outcomes and may be more meaningful than standardized scales.

HEALTH EQUITY CONSIDERATIONS IN MANAGING BPSD

Social determinants of health influence the risk of developing dementia, the experience of BPSD, and access to appropriate care. Racial/ethnic differences in the presentation of BPSD have been observed, and cultural factors influence how behaviors are interpreted and managed. For example, some cultures may view certain behaviors as more or less problematic, influencing whether caregivers seek care and what interventions are considered acceptable.

Access to nonpharmacologic interventions may be limited by socioeconomic status, geographic location, and available resources. Rural communities may have fewer options for specialized dementia care and fewer trained personnel to implement nonpharmacologic interventions. Language barriers can also limit access to appropriate care and support.

Stigma surrounding dementia and mental health may vary across cultures and can influence help-seeking behaviors and treatment choices. Understanding these social determinants of health is essential for developing equitable approaches to BPSD assessment and management.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT OF BPSD MANAGEMENT

Historically, behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia were managed primarily with pharmacologic interventions, particularly antipsychotic medications. In the 1980s and 1990s, there was widespread use of typical and atypical antipsychotics to treat BPSD, despite limited evidence of efficacy and growing concerns about safety.

In the early 2000s, regulatory warnings highlighted the increased risk of mortality associated with antipsychotic use in older adults with dementia. These warnings led to increased scrutiny of antipsychotic prescribing and greater emphasis on nonpharmacologic approaches. However, antipsychotic use remains prevalent in many settings, particularly in long-term care facilities.

Recent guidelines and initiatives have emphasized the importance of person-centered care, nonpharmacologic interventions as first-line treatment, and careful evaluation of medication risks and benefits. The evolution of BPSD management reflects broader shifts toward more holistic and person-centered approaches to dementia care.

PHARMACOLOGIC AND NONPHARMACOLOGIC APPROACHES FOR BPSD

Agitation

Nonpharmacologic interventions for agitation include behavioral interventions, sensory interventions, environmental modifications, and psychological interventions. Music therapy has demonstrated efficacy for reducing agitation in people with dementia. Other effective interventions include caregiver education, structured activities, and environmental modifications to reduce overstimulation.

Pharmacologic options for agitation include atypical antipsychotics, antidepressants, and other medications. However, the evidence supporting pharmacologic interventions for agitation is limited, and the risk of harms (particularly with antipsychotics) is significant. Network meta-analyses have found that some nonpharmacologic interventions have similar or greater efficacy compared to pharmacologic interventions, with fewer adverse effects.

The DICE algorithm (Describe, Investigate, Create, Evaluate) provides a structured approach to managing agitation. This approach emphasizes understanding the meaning behind the behavior, addressing contributing factors, and implementing nonpharmacologic interventions before considering pharmacologic options.

Psychosis

Psychotic symptoms in dementia, including delusions and hallucinations, are challenging to manage. Nonpharmacologic approaches focus on environmental modifications, sensory interventions, and caregiver education. Understanding the meaning behind psychotic symptoms and addressing unmet needs can reduce their frequency and severity.

Pharmacologic options include atypical antipsychotics, which have modest efficacy for psychotic symptoms but carry significant risks. Pimavanserin, a selective serotonin inverse agonist, has been studied for dementia-related psychosis and may have a more favorable safety profile. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine may have modest benefits for psychotic symptoms, but the evidence is limited.

Depression

Depression in dementia is common and associated with poor outcomes. Nonpharmacologic interventions include psychotherapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy, exercise, and reminiscence therapy. Psychological interventions have demonstrated efficacy for reducing depressive symptoms in people with dementia.

Pharmacologic options include antidepressants, but the evidence for their efficacy is mixed. Meta-analyses have found modest benefits of antidepressants for depression in dementia, but the effect sizes are small and the risk of adverse effects is significant. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the most commonly prescribed class.

Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms in dementia are often overlooked but can significantly impact quality of life. Nonpharmacologic interventions include cognitive-behavioral therapy, relaxation techniques, and environmental modifications to reduce stress and anxiety. Psychological interventions have demonstrated efficacy for anxiety in people with dementia.

Pharmacologic options include antidepressants and benzodiazepines, but both have limited evidence and significant risks. Benzodiazepines are associated with falls, confusion, and worsening cognitive function, and their use should be minimized.

Apathy

Apathy is one of the most common neuropsychiatric symptoms in dementia but is often undertreated. Nonpharmacologic interventions include structured activities, exercise, and caregiver education to engage people with dementia in meaningful activities. Behavioral activation has demonstrated efficacy for reducing apathy.

Pharmacologic options include stimulants such as methylphenidate, which has shown modest efficacy in randomized controlled trials. However, the evidence is limited, and the risk of adverse effects must be carefully considered.

Sleep and Circadian Rhythm Disturbances

Sleep disturbances are common in dementia and significantly impact quality of life for people with dementia and their caregivers. Nonpharmacologic interventions include sleep hygiene education, light therapy, physical activity, and environmental modifications. Bright light therapy has demonstrated efficacy for improving sleep and reducing nighttime disturbances.

Pharmacologic options include melatonin, trazodone, and other sleep-promoting medications. However, the evidence is limited, and medications carry risks including falls, confusion, and daytime sedation. Trazodone is commonly used but has modest efficacy and significant adverse effects.

Inappropriate Sexual Behaviors

Inappropriate sexual behaviors are less common but can be distressing for caregivers and challenging to manage. Nonpharmacologic interventions focus on environmental modifications, caregiver education, and understanding the meaning behind the behavior. Evidence for effective interventions is limited.

Pharmacologic options include antipsychotics and other medications, but the evidence is extremely limited and the risks often outweigh benefits. Management typically focuses on nonpharmacologic approaches and addressing contributing factors.

Eating Disturbances

Eating disturbances in dementia include changes in appetite, food preferences, and eating behaviors. Nonpharmacologic interventions include nutritional assessment, environmental modifications to support eating, and caregiver education on feeding assistance. Understanding the meaning behind eating changes is important.

Pharmacologic options are limited and generally not recommended. Management focuses on addressing underlying causes such as dental problems, depression, or medication side effects. Nutritional supplementation may be considered in some cases.

Prescribing Cholinesterase Inhibitors or Memantine for BPSD

Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and memantine are approved for cognitive symptoms of dementia but may have benefits for some BPSD. Meta-analyses have found modest benefits of cholinesterase inhibitors for some neuropsychiatric symptoms, but the evidence is mixed and the effect sizes are small.

These medications may be considered for BPSD, particularly when cognitive symptoms and BPSD co-occur. However, the benefits must be carefully weighed against the risks, and these medications should not be prescribed solely for BPSD in the absence of cognitive symptoms.

Is There a Role for New Alzheimer Disease–Modifying Therapies?

New disease-modifying therapies for Alzheimer disease, including lecanemab and donanemab, target amyloid-beta and have shown benefits for cognitive outcomes. Limited data are available on their effects on BPSD. As these therapies become more widely used, understanding their impact on neuropsychiatric symptoms will be important.

Risk of Harm Associated with Interventions for Treating BPSD

Antipsychotic medications are associated with significant harms in people with dementia, including increased mortality, stroke, falls, and cognitive decline. The number needed to harm for mortality with antipsychotics in dementia is approximately 26, meaning that for every 26 people treated, one additional death occurs.

Other psychotropic medications also carry risks. Antidepressants are associated with falls, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hyponatremia. Benzodiazepines are associated with falls, confusion, dependence, and cognitive decline. The risks of these medications must be carefully weighed against potential benefits.

Nonpharmacologic interventions generally have fewer risks than pharmacologic interventions, but they are not risk-free. Some interventions may cause overstimulation or understimulation, and poorly implemented interventions may fail to address the underlying causes of BPSD. However, overall, nonpharmacologic interventions have a more favorable safety profile than medications.

Deprescribing Pharmacologic Interventions and Deimplementing Nonpharmacologic Interventions

Deprescribing is the planned process of reducing or stopping medications that are no longer beneficial or where the harms outweigh the benefits. For BPSD, deprescribing should focus on antipsychotics and other psychotropic medications that may have been initiated without adequate trials of nonpharmacologic interventions.

Guidelines for deprescribing include gradual tapering, monitoring for withdrawal symptoms or recurrence of BPSD, and implementing nonpharmacologic interventions to support the deprescribing process. Successful deprescribing has been demonstrated in multiple settings, with reductions in antipsychotic use without worsening of BPSD.

Deimplementation of nonpharmacologic interventions refers to stopping interventions that are no longer beneficial or where the evidence does not support continued use. This process is less well-studied than deprescribing but is important to ensure resources are directed toward effective interventions.

INITIATIVES TO IMPROVE QUALITY OF CARE

Multiple initiatives have been developed to improve the quality of BPSD management. These include quality metrics for antipsychotic use in long-term care, educational programs for clinicians and caregivers, and implementation of evidence-based guidelines. These initiatives aim to reduce inappropriate medication use and increase the use of nonpharmacologic interventions.

STRENGTHS OF THE BODY OF LITERATURE SUPPORTING BPSD MANAGEMENT

The literature on BPSD management has several strengths. There is a growing body of randomized controlled trials evaluating both pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions. Network meta-analyses have compared the efficacy of different interventions, providing evidence to guide clinical decision-making. The literature has evolved from focusing primarily on pharmacologic interventions to giving greater attention to nonpharmacologic approaches and person-centered care.

LIMITATIONS OF THE BODY OF LITERATURE SUPPORTING BPSD MANAGEMENT

Despite the growing literature, there are important limitations. Many studies have methodological limitations, including small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and heterogeneous outcome measures. The evidence for some interventions remains limited, and more research is needed to determine the optimal approaches for different BPSD and different populations. There is limited evidence on how to effectively implement nonpharmacologic interventions in routine clinical practice.

EMERGING AREAS OF RESEARCH ON BPSD MANAGEMENT

Emerging areas of research include digital health interventions, personalized medicine approaches to BPSD management, and implementation science to improve the uptake of evidence-based practices. There is growing interest in understanding the biological mechanisms underlying BPSD, which may lead to new targeted therapies. Telehealth and remote monitoring may improve access to BPSD assessment and management, particularly for underserved populations.

SUMMARY

BPSD are common in dementia and have significant impacts on people with dementia and their caregivers. There is growing evidence supporting the efficacy of nonpharmacologic interventions, which should be considered first-line treatment. Pharmacologic interventions, particularly antipsychotics, have limited efficacy and significant risks. Person-centered care approaches that understand the meaning behind behaviors and address contributing factors are essential for effective BPSD management. Future research should focus on improving the evidence base for specific interventions, understanding how to implement nonpharmacologic interventions in routine practice, and developing new approaches that are safe and effective.
