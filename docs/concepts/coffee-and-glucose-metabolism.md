# Coffee and Glucose Metabolism

**Definition:** The association between habitual coffee intake and a more favourable glucose-insulin profile, observed primarily in men in the NFBC1966 cohort, consistent with broader evidence linking coffee to reduced type 2 diabetes risk.

## Mechanism

Paradox: Acute coffee/caffeine transiently **impairs** glucose tolerance, but chronic intake associates with **improved** insulin sensitivity. Resolution:

1. **Acute:** Caffeine activates sympathetic nervous system → adrenaline release → hepatic glucose output ↑ → transient hyperglycaemia
2. **Chronic:** Long-term coffee consumption → improved insulin sensitivity through:
   - Lower adiposity and visceral fat → less insulin resistance
   - Inverse BCAA correlation → improved metabolic signalling
   - Higher SHBG → less androgenic adipose stimulation
   - Anti-inflammatory effects (chlorogenic acids, other polyphenols)
   - Enhanced hepatic glucose uptake over time

## Evidence

- **NFBC1966 (men):** Higher coffee intake correlated with lower fasting insulin, lower HOMA2-IR, higher QUICKI, lower insulin during OGTT
- **NFBC1966 (women):** Weaker, fewer glucose-insulin correlations
- **Observational meta-analyses:** Coffee inversely associated with T2D risk, greatest reduction at 3-4 cups/day
- **Mendelian randomization:** Genetically predicted higher caffeine → reduced T2D risk

## Sex Differences

Men showed a broader favourable glucose-insulin profile; women's associations were weaker and centred on adiposity/inflammation rather than direct glucose-insulin markers. Previous reviews have not identified consistent sex-specific effects — this may be cohort-specific or reflect insufficient power.

## Clinical Significance

- The acute-chronic paradox means coffee's glucose effects depend on timing and habit
- 3-4 cups/day appears optimal for T2D risk reduction
- Sex-specific effects, if real, have implications for dietary recommendations
