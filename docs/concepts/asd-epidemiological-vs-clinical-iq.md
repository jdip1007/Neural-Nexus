# ASD Epidemiological vs Clinical IQ Profiles

**Definition:** The systematic divergence in reported IQ distributions between epidemiological (population-based) and clinical (treatment-seeking) ASD samples, driven by multiple biases.

## Mechanism

Epidemiological studies aim to capture the full ASD population and historically report higher proportions of cognitive impairment (IQ < 70). Clinical studies, sampling from specialized outpatient clinics, report more individuals with average or above-average IQ. Three biases drive this divergence:

1. **Referral bias:** Specialized clinics attract individuals who are "less obviously" autistic — often higher-IQ, later-diagnosed, with subtler presentations
2. **Diagnostic bias:** IQ influences who receives an ASD diagnosis and when; higher-IQ individuals may be missed in childhood
3. **Reporting bias:** Epidemiological studies often collapse or omit above-average IQ data, understating the high-IQ ASD population

## Historical Shift

Early epidemiological studies (1966–2001): 40–100% of ASD with IQ < 70 (mean ~70%)
Recent epidemiological studies: ~31% with IQ < 70, 44% with IQ ≥ 85
Clinical studies: Even higher proportions of average/above-average IQ

This shift reflects both real changes (broadened diagnostic criteria capturing more high-IQ individuals) and methodological improvements.

## Clinical Significance

- Population-level planning should use epidemiological estimates, not clinical samples
- Service design must account for the full IQ spectrum, including high-IQ ASD individuals who may not present to clinics
- Research findings from clinical samples may not generalize to the broader ASD population
